# onetx — a crosswalked O*NET for Singapore, joined to the AI frontier

**What this is.** A data pipeline that builds one connected map of work:

```
O*NET tasks  ──►  detailed / intermediate / generalized work activities  ──►  skills, knowledge, abilities
     │                                                                          ▲
     ▼                                                                          │
O*NET occupations ──► SOC 2018 ──► ISCO-08 ──► SSOC 2024 (Singapore) ──► Skills Framework job roles
                                                                            ├── critical work functions / key tasks  ──► O*NET tasks (text match)
                                                                            └── TSCs / CCS / unique skills            ──► O*NET activities + skills (text match)
```

…and then lays frontier-AI evidence on top of it:

From that it produces, for every task, activity, skill, occupation, SSOC code and Skills Framework role:

* **frontier scores** — how much observed AI use looks like *doing* the work rather than *helping*;
* **timing** — a central year, with a fast and a slow case, by which the item is "disruptable" (AI can do it at workplace reliability *and* typical organisations have had time to adopt it).

Everything is a planning signal with a range. Nothing here forecasts job loss.

## Two ways in

**No code.** The web page built from this pipeline lets anyone search a Singapore job title or Skills Framework role and see which of its tasks AI already does, which stay human, and the range of years by which half the job could be disruptable, with sector, skill and whole-economy views. Published copies: [the map](https://claude.ai/artifact/FC2mCKb7exU2u9jmVbFNPS) and its [companion page](https://claude.ai/artifact/Pxd31ubFiwTQjdTSBsu7qZ) (how it was built, the QA log, and how King and Claude worked together; source in `site/companion.html`). Rebuild the map with `python -m onetx site`, which writes `site/index.html` plus the files it loads on demand: `kt.json` (key tasks per role), `search.json` (alternative titles and the O*NET task index) and the meaning-search model and vectors (`model.*`, `emb.*`, from `onetx/site_search.py`). The page's "Ask Claude" button uses the artifact `sample` capability and only appears inside a Claude viewer. A scenario switch (fast, central, slow) at the top changes every year and curve on the page; each job shows what its years rest on (seen in AI use, rated doable, newly doable, raters disagree, unproven, physical) and an "evidence as of" line gives the date of every input. Every job leads with "AI doing" (the share of each task AI completes, from the usage data or the 2026 rating) beside "AI involved" (whole tasks), with a ceiling line and the year AI could be doing a quarter of the work. Every job page ends with what has actually happened so far: MOM vacancies, wages, employment and retrenchments by occupation, with the map's signals tested against them on every build (`onetx/outcomes.py`). A country switch at the top sets which workforce every tab shows (Singapore, the United States, Australia, the United Kingdom, Canada, New Zealand, Norway, Sweden, Finland, Denmark, the Netherlands, Ireland, Japan, South Korea, Germany or France), and every record carries a depth mark, full, medium or limited, with a sentence on what is coarse or borrowed; the US side carries its own job pages (O*NET tasks by work activity, skills with levels and written examples, software, related occupations, BLS employment and pay, the one-year change), career clusters with the Department of Labor's industry competency models as the stand-in for Skills Framework sectors, and a test of whether AI reaches low-level or high-level work first. Australia, the UK, Canada, New Zealand, Norway, Sweden, Finland, Denmark, the Netherlands, Ireland, Japan, South Korea, Germany and France are built through a shared country layer (`onetx/countries.py`): each has its own occupation pages (the statistics office's official task list matched task by task to O*NET, with the country's own timing), its own position on the adoption curve measured from its own firm survey, industry shifts from the same survey carried through its occupation-by-industry mix, a sectors tab of industries with the survey readings, its national skill levels against AI, and a rung-five test on its own jobs and pay data; the UK also carries Skills England's 712 apprenticeship standards as the Skills Framework stand-in, each duty timed like a key task, grouped into 15 routes; Canada reaches O*NET directly through the official SOC-to-NOC concordance and carries OaSIS, its own O*NET-style system, whose main duties are timed task by task and whose skill ratings give a national check on the O*NET skill-level test; New Zealand shares ANZSCO with Australia and adds the Census's sub-major-group income and occupation-by-industry table through the Stats NZ API, with Australia's adoption reading standing in (marked as borrowed) until Stats NZ publishes its own; Germany and France are built from GENESIS-Online and Melodi with their offices' own correspondences to ISCO-08. Norway sits on ISCO-08 itself, with five yearly firm-survey readings by industry and quarterly register jobs and pay. A "Compare countries" view puts any two of the sixteen side by side: head-to-head figures, both curves on one chart, a split of the gap into job mix and timing, and each country's rung-five test (the same nine occupation groups where both share the ISCO grouping); job pages link to their counterparts in the other countries. A "Europe: 32 countries" tab runs every country in Eurostat's AI survey through the same model at two-digit occupation level, ranked by when AI could be doing a quarter of each wage bill (`onetx/europe.py`). Rebuild the country tables with `python -m onetx countries`, then `python -m onetx forecast` and `python -m onetx site`. A "Reading the news" view places any report about AI and jobs on a five-rung ladder (rated, seen in use, AI mostly doing it, adopted, job changed), translates a report's headline number down the ladder with conversion rates measured on this map's own tasks, and scores every source behind the map on its track record (`onetx/validation.py`).

**Code.** Run the pipeline to rebuild every table, change the assumptions, or query the DuckDB file.

## Quick start

```bash
pip install -e .            # Python 3.11+
python -m onetx fetch       # ~700 MB of public files into data/raw (see docs/DATA_SOURCES.md)
python -m onetx build       # ~5 minutes on a laptop; writes data/processed/*.parquet + onetx.duckdb + docs/REPORT.md
python -m onetx query "select ssoc_code, ssoc_title, half_year_p50 from forecast_ssoc2024 order by 3 limit 10"
python -m onetx rate        # (optional, ~25 min, ~US$15 of Claude use) re-rate every task on onetx/rubric_2026.md; cached in data/reference
python -m onetx forecast    # re-run only the timing model (fast); use after `rate` or to try parameters
python -m onetx site        # the browsable page, from the processed tables
```

Or open `data/processed/onetx.duckdb` in any DuckDB client; every processed table is a view.

## Status

Second version. Compared with the first: four providers instead of one at occupation level (Anthropic, Microsoft, OpenAI, Google) with a consensus count; a per-task barrier index (interpersonal, in-person, accountability, hand-over) in place of a flat lag; an adoption anchor fitted to the US Census firm-adoption series with Singapore's lead applied; and MOM wages (June 2025, coded to SSOC) and employment (2-digit groups) so Singapore results can be read as shares of people and pay. Known gaps are listed at the end of `docs/METHOD.md`.
