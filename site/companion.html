Making the Work and AI Map

The companion to the Singapore Work and AI Map. Three parts: how the map was built, the quality checks that were run on it and what they changed, and a record of how King and Claude worked together to get here.

Written by Claude on 23 October 2026 from the session record, last updated 24 October 2026. Code and documentation: KR48VR/tss2026, branch claude/funny-hypatia-pfz7du.

Part 1

How we did this

The question was King's: build a version of O*NET that cross-walks tasks, activities and skills, link it to Singapore's own databases, lay the frontier AI labs' usage reports on top, and forecast which jobs, skills and tasks are being disrupted and when. Here is how that became a working map, in the order it happened.

1. One connected map of work

O*NET, the US Department of Labor's database, already links 1,016 occupations to 18,796 tasks, 2,087 detailed work activities and 35 skills. What it lacks is Singapore's language and any evidence about AI. We added both.

Official code tables carry the occupation links: O*NET to SOC to ISCO-08 (two independent routes, one through the ESCO crosswalk and one through the BLS table) to SSOC through SingStat's correspondence tables, and SSOC to Skills Framework roles through SingStat's own tagging of those roles in its alphabetical index. That codes 1,556 of the 2,030 roles officially. Where no table exists, a text matcher that blends sentence embeddings with word-overlap links Singapore's key tasks and skills to O*NET's tasks and activities, keeping a score on every link. On the roles SingStat did code, that matcher finds the exact code 37% of the time and has it in its top five 64% of the time, which is why it is only the fallback.

2. The evidence layers

  1. Anthropic Economic Index. Seven monthly snapshots from December 2024 to May 2026 of which O*NET tasks Claude conversations map to, whether the AI does the task or helps with it, how autonomous it is, and how long a person would need. The only source that publishes usage by task.
  2. Anthropic labour-market and robot files. Task penetration, job exposure, and for 7,594 physical tasks a robot-readiness tier.
  3. Microsoft "Working with AI". Bing Copilot usage by intermediate work activity and an applicability score per occupation.
  4. OpenAI. The 2023 "GPTs are GPTs" exposure ratings per occupation, and the 2025 shares of ChatGPT messages by work activity, typed in from the paper.
  5. Google ATLAS. Lists of occupations over- and under-represented in 15 million Gemini conversations, typed in from the July 2026 paper. Google publishes no task table.
  6. METR time horizons. Re-fitted from METR's raw runs: the length of task a frontier model completes at 50% reliability has doubled about every 106 days since 2024.
  7. Adoption. The US Census survey of firms using AI (51 readings since 2023) and Microsoft's measure of people using generative AI by country (Singapore 64%, US 33%).
  8. Singapore people and pay. MOM's June 2025 wage survey, which carries SSOC codes for 523 occupations, and MOM's 2025 Labour Force report, resident employment for 43 occupation sub-groups.

3. From evidence to scores

Every task gets a status: AI doing it (seen in usage data in any release since December 2024 and, when seen, mostly completed by the AI), AI helping, physical, or, for tasks never seen, the strongest remaining evidence that AI can do them: rated doable (by the 2023 human and GPT-4 raters in OpenAI's study and by Claude's October 2026 rating of every task on the same scale), newly rated (only the 2026 rating says yes), raters disagree, or unproven (every rater said no). "Unproven" never means AI cannot; it means nobody has shown it yet. The third round found that the earlier build counted a task as seen only if it appeared in the two newest monthly releases, which list a third as many tasks as the first release did; "seen" now means seen in any release, which is why the headline shares rose. Scores roll up along the map as weighted averages: tasks to activities and occupations by task importance, occupations to SSOC codes by the crosswalk weights, SSOC codes to Skills Framework roles, and roles also directly through their key tasks. Skills are scored through the jobs that need them. As a check, our occupation scores correlate 0.67 with Anthropic's own occupation measure and 0.49 with Microsoft's.

4. From scores to years

The timing model has three parts and always reports a fast, central and slow case.

  1. Capability. AI is already used to do tasks of a few hours; the usage data put today's "delegation horizon" at about 4.3 hours, and most desk tasks are shorter than that. So length alone would call almost every desk task capable now, and the third round replaced that with evidence: a desk task counts as capable today only if AI use on it has been seen, or it is rated doable; unproven tasks wait 1.9 years, disputed ones 1.0 year and tasks only the 2026 rating calls doable 3.1 years (central case), each lag measured from how far behind rated tasks that group is in actually being seen in use. The 2026 rating was produced by Claude for all 18,796 tasks through a re-runnable command and checked against the 2023 raters, against usage, and against a repeat run. Physical tasks follow their robot tier instead.
  2. Adoption. A logistic curve fitted to the US Census survey of firms using AI, now downloaded live on every rebuild (latest reading 4 October 2026, 24.6% of firms) with a step for the Census's late-2025 rewording of the question; it crosses half of US firms in 2029.2 (fast 2027.5, slow 2032.7). Singapore firms run 0.6 years ahead, measured by comparing IMDA's enterprise survey with the US series at matched levels. Each job's industries then move its date, from about 2.1 years earlier for information and communications to 2.0 years later for accommodation and food services, carried from the Census sector series through MOM's industry-by-occupation table and the Skills Framework's sector tags.
  3. Barriers. Each task gets a barrier score from four things: interpersonal work, in-person work, accountability, and how often people keep the task even with AI around. A high score delays adoption by up to four years in the central case.

A job's "half-way year" is the first year by which half of its task weight is disruptable under those assumptions. It means AI could do the work and typical employers have had time to adopt it. It does not mean the job disappears. The page now carries a fast, central and slow switch that changes every year and curve, and an "evidence as of" line with the date of every input.

5. Finding things on the page

Searching went through three versions. The first matched keywords in job titles only. King tested it and asked whether that was all it did; it was. The second added SingStat's 6,755 alternative titles (so "CEO" and "nursemaid" work), tolerance for small typos, and a task search over 40,367 Skills Framework key tasks and 18,796 O*NET tasks. The third added two kinds of AI matching: a small embedding model that runs inside the page and matches by meaning ("someone who manages a hotel" finds hotel operations managers; "look after elderly people at home" finds the care tasks), loaded once on demand; and an "Ask Claude" button that sends the search and the best candidates to Claude on the reader's own account and returns a ranked reading. The in-page model is the same one the pipeline uses to link Singapore's tasks to O*NET, exported to run in a browser; its vectors match the Python original exactly.

6. Two ways in

7. Reopening the honest limits

Two of the four limits named in the first round had been parked: task length barely separates desk jobs, and the adoption lag was an assumption. When the discussion reopened, King asked a question that changed the plan: the sources behind the proposed fixes dated from 2023 and August 2025, so how useful were they, given how fast AI moves? The answer set the rules for the round. An old "yes" still stands and an old "no" is only a flag to look again; a frozen copy of a fortnightly series is a bug, not a source; and every input on the page should carry its date. The stale adoption copy proved the point: refitted to the Census file downloaded that day, the curve had been running a year behind reality. The round then did, in the order judged most robust: a live Census feed with a step for the reworded question; the 2023 task ratings as an evidence tier with a lag measured from usage rather than assumed; a fresh 2026 rating of all 18,796 tasks by Claude, validated three ways; Singapore's own firm-adoption series for the lead; sector-specific adoption from the Census sector series; and the page work: the scenario switch, the evidence bar under every job, and the dates. One result cut against the new rating: tasks that only the 2026 rating calls doable are seen in use less often than tasks it still calls impossible, so they wait the longest lag. The data are allowed to overrule the model.

8. Reading the news

King then asked three questions in one message: what the scenario switch does, why the years carried a decimal, and whether experts' past ratings had any relationship with what later happened. The decimal (a fraction of a year) was replaced with a season after the year, "2031 mid", at King's choice. The third question had a measurable answer, and it became a new view. Every claim about AI and work sits on one rung of a ladder: rated doable, seen in use, AI mostly doing it, adopted by employers, job changed. On this map's own tasks, 42% of the tasks rated doable in 2023 had been seen in use by 2026, and 26% of the ones rated impossible had been seen anyway; among seen tasks, AI mostly does the work in 41%. The "Reading the news" view shows the ladder with those conversions, a translator that takes a report's number and rung and walks it down the ladder, and a scorecard of every source behind the map with its rung, its date and its track record here. King's reason for wanting it: there are many reports, their record is less than stellar, and readers need a way to place each one.

9. "Involved" or "doing"

Explaining the ladder raised a question King asked Claude to put plainly, twice: did "half the job disruptable" mean AI could be involved in half the work, or doing half of it? The map had been counting whole tasks, the first meaning. A ten-task example settled it: AI capable of six tasks means involved in 60%, but if it does nearly all of two, half of two and a fifth of two, it is doing about 30%. King chose "doing" for the headline and asked for it to be easy to see. Claude tested two measures of how much of a task AI does: the time it saves (about 90% for almost every task, so it separates nothing) and the share of AI use where AI completes the task rather than helps (40% to 60%, and it tracks the 2026 end-to-end rating). The second now drives a "doing" line on every chart beside a dashed "involved" line, a ceiling line showing what AI would be doing if every task were disrupted, and three numbers per job: AI's share of the work by 2035, the year AI could be doing a quarter of it, and the year AI could be involved in half. The ceiling is under half for most jobs, which is why the headline year is "a quarter": on today's patterns AI helps with more than it does.

10. What has happened so far

Asked what came next, Claude proposed five steps and recommended the one that tests the forecast against reality rather than against other predictions: the ladder's fifth rung, the job market itself. King chose it. MOM turned out to publish more than expected: job vacancies by detailed occupation for September 2023, 2024 and 2025, employed residents by occupation sub-group every June since 2011, median wages for two years, and retrenchment, vacancy and resignation rates by occupational group. The 2024 vacancy file only served under a different spelling of its name, the same server quirk as the wage files. Every job page now ends with "What has happened so far": its vacancies, its wage change, its sub-group's employment line since 2011, and its group's retrenchment rate. The map's signals are tested against these outcomes on every build. The result is a baseline, not a verdict: across 452 occupations, wage growth and vacancy shares show no clear relation to exposure; across occupation groups, the more exposed groups have grown faster since 2019, the opposite of what disruption would show; retrenchments per 1,000 PMETs rose from 6.1 in 2019 to 10.0 in 2025 while falling for other groups. The forecast's central years mostly start in 2029, so this is what the next releases will be judged against.

11. A tab for leaders

King asked for an executive summary on the map itself: how the work built on and improved O*NET, Singapore's data and the AI companies' reports. The new "For leaders" tab answers that in three cards (what exists, what we added, why it matters), a one-breath summary and six tiles that are live from the build, a "how far to trust it" list with the dates and track-record numbers, four uses, and a plain statement of what the map is not. Nothing on it is typed in by hand: every figure is read from the same data as the rest of the page, so it cannot drift from the detail views.

King then asked for the summary to come first, for the scenario switch to be explained where it sits, and for plainer names for the three settings. The executive summary is now the landing tab; the switch reads "Fast 1.5× pace", "Central measured pace", "Slow 0.6× pace", with the multipliers read from the model; and a line under it says what each setting does to adoption, waiting times and barriers, and which to use for planning, stress tests and budgets. The summary's own figures follow the switch.

12. Colour and shape

King found the page read like a wall of text, and pointed at one symptom: the three scenario buttons looked the same, so nothing said they could be chosen. Claude proposed a six-point design pass and King approved it. Each scenario now has its own colour (orange for fast, teal for central, blue for slow) that runs through the button, a band along the top of the page, the "AI doing" line on every chart and the shaded fast-to-slow range, so a reader always knows which setting the numbers belong to; the chosen button is filled and ticked. Years in the tables sit on warm-to-cool shading, so early years stand out without reading every cell. Each evidence tier has a fixed colour across pills, bars and legends. The executive summary opens with the economy's own chart and a small staircase of the evidence ladder; the three source cards carry an icon and coloured steps for what existed, what was added and why it matters. Long method passages fold behind headings the reader opens when needed, and the one-line summaries sit in tinted boxes.

The first build failed its own accessibility check twice: the orange was a shade too light for the unselected fast button, and in dark mode white text on the light fills was unreadable. Both were fixed by darkening the orange and using dark text on dark-mode fills. The screenshots then showed the ladder's labels overlapping, the summary saying "2031" while its chart said "2030 mid", and the phone header eating a third of the screen. The ladder was relaid with the conversion rates in the free space above each step, every year on the page now uses the same year-and-season wording, the scenario explanation folds away on phones and the tabs scroll in one row.

13. Three scenarios side by side

Looking at the new summary, King asked three things: make it clear there are three scenarios to choose from; say what happens if a reader wants to compare them; and explain the empty space to the right of the text. The space was a reading-width limit on the intro and the one-breath summary, not a plan, and comparing meant flipping the switch and remembering. Claude used the space for a "three scenarios, one switch" panel beside the summary: one row per scenario in its own colour, with the year a quarter of the wage bill is done, AI doing by 2035 and AI involved by 2035, the chosen row ticked, and each row clickable as a second switch. A "show all three on the chart" box draws the fast, central and slow lines together on the economy chart, with a dot where each reaches a quarter; the same box sits under every job's timeline, so a single job can be compared the same way. The one-breath summary no longer repeats the three years, since they are in the table beside it.

King then pointed at the chart's legend: "AI doing (slow case)", "AI involved", "ceiling on today's patterns", "fast to slow band" were shorthand from the model, and nothing beside the chart said what they meant. Claude explained each in the reply and made every chart explain itself: the legend now shows the line style rather than a coloured square (solid, dashed, dotted, shaded), says in plain words what each is, the lines are labelled "doing" and "involved" at their ends, and a short "reading the chart" note under each chart says what the solid line, the dashed line, the shaded range and the red ceiling are, naming the subject (Singapore's total pay, this occupation's work, this role's work) and the ceiling figure.

King's next screenshot showed the compare view with "fast", "central" and "slow" all printed in the same dark grey, so nothing tied the words to the orange, green and blue lines. The cause was one page-wide style rule that set the colour of every piece of text inside a chart, overriding the colours the chart asked for; it had also been silently turning the red "ceiling" label and the "doing" and "involved" labels dark since they were added. The rule now applies only to text with no colour of its own. In the compare view each label is also joined to its line by a short leader, and on wide screens each dot carries its case and year ("fast 2027 late"), so the tie does not depend on colour alone.

14. A second country

King asked whether the map could compare across cities and countries, starting with Singapore and the United States. The task-level model never depended on Singapore: what AI can do, how much of each task it does, the barriers and the evidence lags are the same everywhere. Three things differ by country: who does which jobs and what they are paid, where the country sits on the adoption curve, and which industries its occupations sit in. Claude built the United States as a second workforce on exactly those three inputs: the Bureau of Labor Statistics' employment and wages for every occupation (O*NET's own codes, so no crosswalk is needed; occupations the BLS publishes only in combination take the remainder of their group), the US adoption curve itself with no lead, and the Census sector shifts carried through the BLS occupation-by-sector mix instead of Singapore's. The timing model is then run a second time and weighted by the US workforce through the same code that weights Singapore's.

The new "Singapore vs US" tab puts the two side by side: a head-to-head table, both "AI doing" curves on one chart, the same nine occupation groups for both, and a split of the gap into a job-mix part and a timing part, measured by running each country's workforce on the other's timing, so the two parts add up to the whole gap. The US gets its own rung-five test, the one-year employment and wage change by occupation from May 2023 to May 2024, read with the same rules as Singapore's. Every Singapore job page now shows its US counterpart: how many are employed, the median pay, and the year AI could be doing a quarter of the work there against here. Adding a third country means supplying the same three inputs.

15. Two countries on one page

King asked for sections for all of Singapore's data, sections for all of the US's, room for more countries, and the comparison kept. Claude proposed a country switch next to the scenario switch rather than a second set of tabs: the same tabs stay, and the summary, overview, job search and sectors follow the chosen country, while the task-level views (skills, reading the news, how to read this) are shared and the "Singapore vs US" tab compares. King agreed, then asked what in O*NET could stand in for the Skills Framework on the US side. Claude went through the O*NET download file by file and matched each piece of the Framework: occupations and 54,000 job titles for roles; core task statements with importance ratings for key tasks; work activities for critical work functions; knowledge, skills and abilities rated for importance and for the level needed, with written examples at each level, for competencies and proficiency; Job Zones and related occupations for career levels and moves; software with hot-technology flags; emerging tasks. The one missing piece was the sector framework itself.

Two stand-ins for sectors were out of reach at first: O*NET's website refused the automated fetcher, and so did the Department of Labor's competency model site. King asked Claude to try again; a browser-style request worked for both. The 14 career clusters were collected from O*NET's pages (997 of 1,016 occupations), and the Department of Labor turned out to publish its whole Competency Model Clearinghouse as a data download: 30 industry models, 1,020 competency blocks, 5,792 key behaviours. King asked whether O*NET's skill and knowledge levels would be used in place of proficiency levels; Claude said yes, in three places, and King said go ahead and make the subtleties easy to see.

What was built: US occupation pages with tasks grouped by work activity (core tasks marked, importance shown), the skills and knowledge the job needs with the level and a written example, software, where the jobs are, related occupations, BLS employment and pay, the one-year change in employment and wages, and the Singapore counterpart; a US "Sectors" tab on the 14 clusters, each opening to its largest occupations and the industry's competency blocks with key behaviours, and a note on how the three pieces stand in for the Framework; a shared "skill levels and AI" panel on the Skills tab that groups occupations by the level a skill needs and reads the result in plain words (AI is seen most in work needing high levels of desk skills, least in hands-on skills; by 2035 the most AI-done work sits in the middle band, because the highest-level work carries more of the barriers); the executive summary, overview and job search rewritten per country; and the US sub-groups (113 BLS minor groups) beside Singapore's 43.

16. Two more countries

King asked whether the two caveats on the US side (no proficiency level per competency; levels describe today's job, not tomorrow's) were on the page. They were, inside the explanations; Claude moved both into the visible introductions so no click is needed. King then asked which other country had data comprehensive enough to show what this kind of analysis can do. Claude ranked Australia first (quarterly employment by detailed occupation, an earnings survey by occupation, a business survey with an AI question, and a classification that publishes a task list and skill level for every unit group), then the UK (employee jobs and pay by SOC 2020 unit group, a fortnightly business survey that asks which AI technologies firms currently use, a Census table of occupation by industry, and Skills England's apprenticeship standards, the closest thing another country has to the Skills Framework), then Canada. King said "shall we do australia and UK?", and Claude took Australia first.

The reconnaissance found what was reachable: the ABS site and the ONS site served their files to a browser-style request, the ABS classification pages carried the "Tasks Include" list for every unit group (364 pages collected), and the apprenticeship standards came from a public API with every duty, knowledge, skill and behaviour statement. Two things were out of reach: Jobs and Skills Australia, which holds the Australian Skills Classification, refused every connection, and the UK's employment-by-occupation series had been discontinued in 2018, so the UK's weights are ASHE employee jobs, which also carry the pay. Both are recorded on the page and in the method document. The UK survey's wave files turned out to carry all the earlier waves in one time-series sheet, so one download gave thirteen readings from 9% of firms in late 2023 to 30% in late 2026; Australia's survey has two readings three years apart (1% and 12%), so its position on the adoption curve rests on one generative-AI-era point and says so.

Rather than a third and fourth copy of the US code, Claude wrote one shared country layer: a short record per country (its classification, loaders, labels) and the same eight steps for each: the crosswalk through ISCO-08 (the ABS correspondence table for Australia; the ONS coding index, which gives an ISCO code for each of its 32,670 job titles, for the UK), the weights, the lead read off the US curve the way Singapore's is, industry shifts from the country's own survey carried through its occupation-by-industry mix, the timing run and the job-mix / timing split against every other country, the rung-five test on the country's own jobs and pay data, exposure by the national skill level (ANZSCO 1 to 5, SOC 1 to 4), and the official task list of every unit group matched to O*NET tasks with the matcher built for Singapore's SSOC task bullets. For the UK the apprenticeship duties were matched the same way, so a standard reads like a Skills Framework role with each duty timed. A fifth country is now a record and two loaders.

On the page the country switch grew to four buttons; the "Singapore vs US" tab became "Compare countries" with a picker for any pair, the head-to-head table, chart, gap split and outcomes following the pair (the nine-group table appears only where both countries share the ISCO grouping, and says so otherwise); each Australian and UK job page shows the statistics office's own description and task list with the AI label, year and match score on each task, the employment and pay, the one- or two-year change, the skill level in words, the US and Singapore counterparts, and for the UK the apprenticeship standards that lead to the job, each opening to its duties and its knowledge, skills and behaviours; the Sectors tab shows the industry divisions with the survey's AI-use readings and the timing shift each implies, plus the 15 apprenticeship routes for the UK; and the Skills tab gained a national skill-level panel beside the O*NET one. Along the way one older bug surfaced: the US occupation pages had been showing task years from Singapore's run rather than the US one; each country's task forecast is now saved and used.

17. Canada, and a national O*NET

King asked which other countries could be done. Claude ranked them by what their statistics offices publish: Canada and New Zealand could go to the same depth as Australia and the UK; Ireland and the Nordic countries have detailed occupation data but thinner skills systems; the rest of Europe is reachable through Eurostat at a coarser level; Japan and Korea would need language work; Hong Kong and Taiwan are thin. King said "ok please do your recommendation in order", and Claude took Canada first.

Canada turned out to be the richest of the five. Statistics Canada does not publish its employment-by-occupation tables as files but through a web service that answers one cell at a time, so Claude wrote a small client that pulls the 2021 Census counts of employment and income for every NOC 2021 unit group, the Census table of occupation by industry and the quarterly job vacancy survey, 250 cells per request. The business conditions survey gave three yearly readings of the share of firms using AI (6.1% in mid-2024, 12.2% in 2025, 19.2% in 2026) by industry. Two things made Canada different from the earlier countries. First, no ISCO step: Statistics Canada publishes a concordance from the US occupation codes to its own, so every O*NET occupation reaches its Canadian unit group directly (1,956 links covering all 516 groups). Second, OaSIS, the Occupational and Skills Information System from Employment and Social Development Canada, is in effect a Canadian O*NET: for every unit group it lists the main duties (4,965 in all), 18,654 example and alternative titles, and the level each of 35 skills, 42 work activities and 33 knowledge areas is needed at. The duties matched O*NET tasks better than the Australian or UK lists (a mean match score of 0.54 against 0.46 and 0.44), because they are written in O*NET's style.

The ratings allowed a check the earlier countries could not give. The O*NET skill-level test asks whether AI reaches the jobs that need high skill levels first, but its levels are American ratings carried across the crosswalk. OaSIS rates the same Canadian jobs with Canadian analysts, so Claude ran the same test with OaSIS's levels and placed it beside the O*NET one in the Skills tab. Both point the same way: across 511 unit groups the rank correlation between the average skill level a job needs and "AI doing by 2035" is 0.39 with OaSIS's ratings and 0.58 with O*NET's, and the NOC's own TEER categories agree (the management and degree-level groups are the most exposed). The pattern is not an artefact of US ratings. On the job pages each Canadian job shows OaSIS's duties with the AI label and year on each, the skills and knowledge OaSIS says it needs, and the O*NET occupations it was built from.

In the results Canadian firms sit about half a year behind the US adoption curve, with industries from nearly three years ahead (information and cultural industries, finance, professional services) to nearly four behind (agriculture); AI could be doing a third of the Canadian wage bill's work by 2035 in the central case and a quarter of it by mid-2032. The rung-five test used the vacancy survey rather than employment, because the Labour Force Survey does not publish unit-group employment: vacancies fell 5.5% in the four quarters to mid-2026 against the four before, and fell in every exposure band (3.5% in the least exposed third, 6.0% in the most), so the verdict stays "not yet". One caveat is on the page and in the method document: the Census weights are from May 2021 and the 2020 income year, older than the other countries', because that is the only source of employment by unit group. Two small things surfaced in QA and were fixed before publishing: the vacancy figures had been labelled "thousand" like the other countries' employment, and Job Bank's hourly wages had been shown as yearly.

18. New Zealand, on open data only

New Zealand was next in the order. It shares ANZSCO with Australia, so the correspondence table, the ABS task lists, the title index and the skill levels all applied unchanged, and the 2023 Census gives employed people for every unit group (2.6 million across 358 groups, the same codes the Australian build carries). Three things were not there. Pay by occupation is published openly only by major group (eight median weekly figures from the Household Labour Force Survey), so every unit group carries its major group's median and the page says so on each job. The Census income table by unit group and the occupation-by-industry table exist in Stats NZ's data explorer, but its API needs a registered key, which this build did not have; the loader is written to use both the moment the key is supplied. And no New Zealand survey has yet asked firms whether they use AI: Stats NZ's first Survey of Business Operations with that question was announced in April 2026 and reports later in the year. Claude's ranking had assumed the business survey carried an AI question, and it did not.

Rather than leave the country out or quietly borrow a number, the country record gained a "lead proxy": Australia's measured position on the adoption curve stands in for New Zealand's, and every place the lead appears (the leaders tab, the evidence line, the compare view) says it is borrowed. No industry shifts are applied, and the Sectors tab explains why there is no industry lens instead of showing an empty table. The rung-five test uses what is open: the 2018-to-2023 Census change per unit group, which grew 7.9% overall and fastest in the most exposed third (15.7% against a 1.1% fall in the least exposed), so the verdict stays "not yet". Stats NZ warns that the 2023 Census changed its occupation coding, and the page repeats the warning.

19. Norway, and Europe at a glance

For Europe, Claude probed the open APIs of Eurostat, Statistics Sweden and Statistics Norway in one pass. Norway won the detailed build: its register of employees and earnings gives employees and median monthly pay for every four-digit occupation every quarter, on STYRK-08, which uses ISCO-08's own codes, so O*NET reaches the 400 occupations with no national crosswalk at all; and its survey of ICT use in enterprises has asked about AI every year since 2021 by industry (11% of firms in 2021, 9% in 2023, 21% in 2024, 30% in 2025, 48% in 2026), the longest series on the map. 22. Are the leads believable? Comparing like with like

King looked at the leads column and did not believe it: Danish firms three years ahead of American ones, Swedish and Finnish firms two and a half. Claude's answer was that the column compared different things. The US figure is the Census question, asked every two weeks of firms of every size: did the business use AI in producing goods or services in the last two weeks? The European offices survey only enterprises with ten or more employees and ask which AI technologies the enterprise uses, from a list; a firm that ticks one box counts. Japan's survey covers enterprises with a hundred or more employees and asks about IoT and AI together. Larger firms adopt sooner and a looser question counts more firms, so the European and Japanese readings sit above the US curve for reasons that are not a lead. King agreed to a two-part fix.

The first part measures like against like. The Census publishes its question by employment-size class, from one-to-four employees up to 250 or more, so Claude built two further US curves: one for firms with ten or more employees and one for firms with a hundred or more, each the firm-weighted mean of its size bands, with the weights from the Census's own count of employer firms by size. The size file only starts with the reworded question of November 2025, too short to carry a growth rate of its own, so the two curves hold the national growth rate and take only their level from the size bands, the same device the sector curves use. Every country is now read against the curve for the firms its own survey covers. The result was smaller than the suspicion: US firms with ten or more employees run only about 0.2 years ahead of all US firms, because most of them have between ten and fifty employees and adopt at nearly the all-firm rate; the European leads each shrank by that much. US firms with a hundred or more employees run 1.3 years ahead, so Japan's lead fell from 1.5 years to 0.2. The headline shares moved little: a point or less for the European records, two points for Japan (AI doing 38% of the wage bill by 2035 instead of 40%, a quarter of it by late 2031 instead of 2030).

The second part is honesty about the rest. No survey asks both questions of the same firms, so the gap between a checklist and the production question cannot be corrected, only bounded. Every lead read from a looser question is now shown as an upper bound, "at most", with the all-firm figure beside it, and each country's record says what its survey covers and asks. Canada's survey asks the Census question over twelve months, so its reading (about half a year behind) is the one fair measurement; Singapore's IMDA figure asks about any AI use in the year, so Singapore's 0.6 years is an upper bound too. The summary tab gained a chart that makes the point visible: each country's own survey readings as dots against the three US curves, with the dots that come from the looser question marked. King then asked for the premise of the scenario switch again, and the answer came from the record: the model had fast and slow cases from the start because every input is uncertain, and the switch exists so that one choice about pace moves every year and curve on the page together.

King also asked, while the fix was running, for a thorough QA and for the page to be easier to read: walls of text broken up, points as bullets, numbers as charts where they help. The "How to read this" tab was rebuilt around short panels and lists, with two small visuals: a strip of ten boxes that shows the difference between AI being involved in six tasks and doing three tasks' worth of work, and a row of boxes that shows how a task's year is built (the US adoption year, less the country's lead, plus or minus its industries, plus the wait for capability, plus the barrier delay). The three kinds of interaction, with examples

King worked with Claude as a tool

King named an outcome and Claude produced it without further discussion. Fourteen of the twenty-nine interactions were of this kind, and they carried most of the volume of work.

  • "please pull in the wages and employment data" (message 3) became tasks 14, 17 and 18: three tables found, 523 occupations coded to wages, 43 employment groups.
  • "can we try to get the downloads again?" (message 4) became task 17: three approaches tried, the third worked.
  • "do thorough QA" (message 6) became tasks 21 and 22: five kinds of check, nine fixes, zero remaining accessibility violations.
  • "Both. And can I search by tasks too?" (message 8) became tasks 25 and 26: alternative titles, typo tolerance and a task index of 59,000 entries, in one round.
  • "which a small language model are we using?" (message 10) became task 31: a plain explanation of the in-page model, its size, and its blind spots such as negation.

King and Claude worked as teammates

King asked a question or made a judgment, Claude answered with reasoning rather than output, and the exchange changed the plan. Five interactions, and they are the ones that shaped the work most.

  • "Wat do the honest limits mean?" (message 2). Claude's plain-language answer turned four caveats into the four-point agenda for the next two rounds.
  • "For 2 and 3, what do you suggest we do?" (message 3). Claude proposed a barrier index and a data-anchored adoption curve and built a first version; King later decided to park the discussion until 4 was done.
  • "Is 1 better now?" (message 4). Claude's answer was "half": a candid assessment King could not have got by reading the code.
  • "Let's come back to 2 and 3 after we do 4." (message 4). King set the order; Claude followed it.
  • "is it easier for the lay person if you create an artifact ... We can offer both options" (message 5). A question, an agreement, and a design decision: a searchable page rather than a fixed dashboard.
  • "When I search for an job, is it found by keywod matching" (message 7). King had used the page and probed exactly how it worked; the plain answer ("yes, and here is where that fails") produced the next round of work.
  • "can it matching be done using gen ai matching?" (message 8). An open question about what was feasible; the answer was two kinds of AI matching, one that runs inside the page and one that asks Claude, and both were built.
  • "how useful are these given how fast AI is mving?" (message 11) was the sharpest exchange of the whole project. It did not ask for a feature; it questioned the evidence behind the plan. The answer changed three things at once: old ratings became flags rather than verdicts, the adoption series became a live feed with a date on the page, and a fresh rating was added with its own reliability test. "Go ahead ... in the sequence you assess is the most robust and rigorous" (message 12) then handed Claude the ordering, which is a teammate move in the other direction: trust extended on the strength of the previous answer.

Each worked independently

Most of Claude's time was spent alone: six stretches, each ending in a pushed commit and a summary. Examples of decisions made without asking:

  • Using O*NET 30.1 as the spine and mapping 31.0's renumbered ids back by name (task 4), because every AI dataset still uses the old ids.
  • Combining two routes from O*NET to ISCO-08 and recording where they agree (task 5).
  • Throwing away the first timing model when it said every desk task was capable now, and recalibrating on observed delegation (task 9).
  • Trying a different spelling of a file name after two cleverer approaches failed (task 17).
  • Choosing a 4-million-parameter embedding model for in-page meaning search, after sizing the 2-million and 8-million alternatives against the page's download budget (task 27).
  • Round three was the longest independent stretch so far: tasks 34 to 38, about two hours of building, rating and testing between message 12 and the report back, with no exchange in between.

King's independent stretches sit between the messages: reading the report after round one, the limits explanation, the second-round summary, the page itself. They are inferred from what each next message picked up on, for example message 4 referring to "1" and "4" by number from the earlier list.

What the pattern says

By count, Claude executed 50 of the 65 tasks and worked alone for most of the elapsed time. By influence, the fifteen teammate exchanges mattered most: every one redirected the work, and four of them ("is 1 better now?", "offer both options", "is it found by keyword matching", "how useful are these given how fast AI is moving?") improved the honesty or reach of the result in ways the tool-style requests did not. A pattern worth naming: King's sharpest questions came after he had used the output himself. The tool-style requests were efficient because they were specific and came with a yes-or-no test of completion, such as whether the download succeeded. The open items King chose to defer, the model weaknesses 2 and 3, are still waiting for the teammate conversation he asked to return to.
