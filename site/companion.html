Making the Work and AI Map

The companion to the Singapore Work and AI Map. Three parts: how the map was built, the quality checks that were run on it and what they changed, and a record of how King and Claude worked together to get here.

Written by Claude on 23 October 2026 from the session record, last updated 24 October 2026. Code and documentation: KR48VR/tss2026, branch claude/funny-hypatia-pfz7du.

Part 1

How we did this

The question was King's: build a version of O*NET that cross-walks tasks, activities and skills, link it to Singapore's own databases, lay the frontier AI labs' usage reports on top, and forecast which jobs, skills and tasks are being disrupted and when. Here is how that became a working map, in the order it happened.

1. One connected map of work

O*NET, the US Department of Labor's database, already links 1,016 occupations to 18,796 tasks, 2,087 detailed work activities and 35 skills. What it lacks is Singapore's language and any evidence about AI. We added both.

Official code tables carry the occupation links: O*NET to SOC to ISCO-08 (two independent routes, one through the ESCO crosswalk and one through the BLS table) to SSOC through SingStat's correspondence tables, and SSOC to Skills Framework roles through SingStat's own tagging of those roles in its alphabetical index. That codes 1,556 of the 2,030 roles officially. Where no table exists, a text matcher that blends sentence embeddings with word-overlap links Singapore's key tasks and skills to O*NET's tasks and activities, keeping a score on every link. On the roles SingStat did code, that matcher finds the exact code 37% of the time and has it in its top five 64% of the time, which is why it is only the fallback.

2. The evidence layers

  1. Anthropic Economic Index. Seven monthly snapshots from December 2024 to May 2026 of which O*NET tasks Claude conversations map to, whether the AI does the task or helps with it, how autonomous it is, and how long a person would need. The only source that publishes usage by task.
  2. Anthropic labour-market and robot files. Task penetration, job exposure, and for 7,594 physical tasks a robot-readiness tier.
  3. Microsoft "Working with AI". Bing Copilot usage by intermediate work activity and an applicability score per occupation.
  4. OpenAI. The 2023 "GPTs are GPTs" exposure ratings per occupation, and the 2025 shares of ChatGPT messages by work activity, typed in from the paper.
  5. Google ATLAS. Lists of occupations over- and under-represented in 15 million Gemini conversations, typed in from the July 2026 paper. Google publishes no task table.
  6. METR time horizons. Re-fitted from METR's raw runs: the length of task a frontier model completes at 50% reliability has doubled about every 106 days since 2024.
  7. Adoption. The US Census survey of firms using AI (51 readings since 2023) and Microsoft's measure of people using generative AI by country (Singapore 64%, US 33%).
  8. Singapore people and pay. MOM's June 2025 wage survey, which carries SSOC codes for 523 occupations, and MOM's 2025 Labour Force report, resident employment for 43 occupation sub-groups.

3. From evidence to scores

Every task gets a status: AI doing it (seen in usage data in any release since December 2024 and, when seen, mostly completed by the AI), AI helping, physical, or, for tasks never seen, the strongest remaining evidence that AI can do them: rated doable (by the 2023 human and GPT-4 raters in OpenAI's study and by Claude's October 2026 rating of every task on the same scale), newly rated (only the 2026 rating says yes), raters disagree, or unproven (every rater said no). "Unproven" never means AI cannot; it means nobody has shown it yet. The third round found that the earlier build counted a task as seen only if it appeared in the two newest monthly releases, which list a third as many tasks as the first release did; "seen" now means seen in any release, which is why the headline shares rose. Scores roll up along the map as weighted averages: tasks to activities and occupations by task importance, occupations to SSOC codes by the crosswalk weights, SSOC codes to Skills Framework roles, and roles also directly through their key tasks. Skills are scored through the jobs that need them. As a check, our occupation scores correlate 0.67 with Anthropic's own occupation measure and 0.49 with Microsoft's.

4. From scores to years

The timing model has three parts and always reports a fast, central and slow case.

  1. Capability. AI is already used to do tasks of a few hours; the usage data put today's "delegation horizon" at about 4.3 hours, and most desk tasks are shorter than that. So length alone would call almost every desk task capable now, and the third round replaced that with evidence: a desk task counts as capable today only if AI use on it has been seen, or it is rated doable; unproven tasks wait 1.9 years, disputed ones 1.0 year and tasks only the 2026 rating calls doable 3.1 years (central case), each lag measured from how far behind rated tasks that group is in actually being seen in use. The 2026 rating was produced by Claude for all 18,796 tasks through a re-runnable command and checked against the 2023 raters, against usage, and against a repeat run. Physical tasks follow their robot tier instead.
  2. Adoption. A logistic curve fitted to the US Census survey of firms using AI, now downloaded live on every rebuild (latest reading 4 October 2026, 24.6% of firms) with a step for the Census's late-2025 rewording of the question; it crosses half of US firms in 2029.2 (fast 2027.5, slow 2032.7). Singapore firms run 0.6 years ahead, measured by comparing IMDA's enterprise survey with the US series at matched levels. Each job's industries then move its date, from about 2.1 years earlier for information and communications to 2.0 years later for accommodation and food services, carried from the Census sector series through MOM's industry-by-occupation table and the Skills Framework's sector tags.
  3. Barriers. Each task gets a barrier score from four things: interpersonal work, in-person work, accountability, and how often people keep the task even with AI around. A high score delays adoption by up to four years in the central case.

A job's "half-way year" is the first year by which half of its task weight is disruptable under those assumptions. It means AI could do the work and typical employers have had time to adopt it. It does not mean the job disappears. The page now carries a fast, central and slow switch that changes every year and curve, and an "evidence as of" line with the date of every input.

5. Finding things on the page

Searching went through three versions. The first matched keywords in job titles only. King tested it and asked whether that was all it did; it was. The second added SingStat's 6,755 alternative titles (so "CEO" and "nursemaid" work), tolerance for small typos, and a task search over 40,367 Skills Framework key tasks and 18,796 O*NET tasks. The third added two kinds of AI matching: a small embedding model that runs inside the page and matches by meaning ("someone who manages a hotel" finds hotel operations managers; "look after elderly people at home" finds the care tasks), loaded once on demand; and an "Ask Claude" button that sends the search and the best candidates to Claude on the reader's own account and returns a ranked reading. The in-page model is the same one the pipeline uses to link Singapore's tasks to O*NET, exported to run in a browser; its vectors match the Python original exactly.

6. Two ways in

The Python pipeline (python -m onetx fetch, build, forecast, site) rebuilds everything in about four minutes from the public files, writes 80 tables and a DuckDB file, and regenerates the web page. The page is the no-code way in: search a job, see its tasks, read the range.

7. Reopening the honest limits

Two of the four limits named in the first round had been parked: task length barely separates desk jobs, and the adoption lag was an assumption. When the discussion reopened, King asked a question that changed the plan: the sources behind the proposed fixes dated from 2023 and August 2025, so how useful were they, given how fast AI moves? The answer set the rules for the round. An old "yes" still stands and an old "no" is only a flag to look again; a frozen copy of a fortnightly series is a bug, not a source; and every input on the page should carry its date. The stale adoption copy proved the point: refitted to the Census file downloaded that day, the curve had been running a year behind reality. The round then did, in the order judged most robust: a live Census feed with a step for the reworded question; the 2023 task ratings as an evidence tier with a lag measured from usage rather than assumed; a fresh 2026 rating of all 18,796 tasks by Claude, validated three ways; Singapore's own firm-adoption series for the lead; sector-specific adoption from the Census sector series; and the page work: the scenario switch, the evidence bar under every job, and the dates. One result cut against the new rating: tasks that only the 2026 rating calls doable are seen in use less often than tasks it still calls impossible, so they wait the longest lag. The data are allowed to overrule the model.

8. Reading the news

King then asked three questions in one message: what the scenario switch does, why the years carried a decimal, and whether experts' past ratings had any relationship with what later happened. The decimal (a fraction of a year) was replaced with a season after the year, "2031 mid", at King's choice. The third question had a measurable answer, and it became a new view. Every claim about AI and work sits on one rung of a ladder: rated doable, seen in use, AI mostly doing it, adopted by employers, job changed. On this map's own tasks, 42% of the tasks rated doable in 2023 had been seen in use by 2026, and 26% of the ones rated impossible had been seen anyway; among seen tasks, AI mostly does the work in 41%. The "Reading the news" view shows the ladder with those conversions, a translator that takes a report's number and rung and walks it down the ladder, and a scorecard of every source behind the map with its rung, its date and its track record here. King's reason for wanting it: there are many reports, their record is less than stellar, and readers need a way to place each one.

9. "Involved" or "doing"

Explaining the ladder raised a question King asked Claude to put plainly, twice: did "half the job disruptable" mean AI could be involved in half the work, or doing half of it? The map had been counting whole tasks, the first meaning. A ten-task example settled it: AI capable of six tasks means involved in 60%, but if it does nearly all of two, half of two and a fifth of two, it is doing about 30%. King chose "doing" for the headline and asked for it to be easy to see. Claude tested two measures of how much of a task AI does: the time it saves (about 90% for almost every task, so it separates nothing) and the share of AI use where AI completes the task rather than helps (40% to 60%, and it tracks the 2026 end-to-end rating). The second now drives a "doing" line on every chart beside a dashed "involved" line, a ceiling line showing what AI would be doing if every task were disrupted, and three numbers per job: AI's share of the work by 2035, the year AI could be doing a quarter of it, and the year AI could be involved in half. The ceiling is under half for most jobs, which is why the headline year is "a quarter": on today's patterns AI helps with more than it does.

10. What has happened so far

Asked what came next, Claude proposed five steps and recommended the one that tests the forecast against reality rather than against other predictions: the ladder's fifth rung, the job market itself. King chose it. MOM turned out to publish more than expected: job vacancies by detailed occupation for September 2023, 2024 and 2025, employed residents by occupation sub-group every June since 2011, median wages for two years, and retrenchment, vacancy and resignation rates by occupational group. The 2024 vacancy file only served under a different spelling of its name, the same server quirk as the wage files. Every job page now ends with "What has happened so far": its vacancies, its wage change, its sub-group's employment line since 2011, and its group's retrenchment rate. The map's signals are tested against these outcomes on every build. The result is a baseline, not a verdict: across {o['n_ssoc_with_wages_both_years']} occupations, wage growth and vacancy shares show no clear relation to exposure; across occupation groups, the more exposed groups have grown faster since 2019, the opposite of what disruption would show; retrenchments per 1,000 PMETs rose from {o['retrenchment']['pmet']['2019']} in 2019 to {o['retrenchment']['pmet']['2025']} in 2025 while falling for other groups. The forecast's central years mostly start in 2029, so this is what the next releases will be judged against.

11. A tab for leaders

King asked for an executive summary on the map itself: how the work built on and improved O*NET, Singapore's data and the AI companies' reports. The new "For leaders" tab answers that in three cards (what exists, what we added, why it matters), a one-breath summary and six tiles that are live from the build, a "how far to trust it" list with the dates and track-record numbers, four uses, and a plain statement of what the map is not. Nothing on it is typed in by hand: every figure is read from the same data as the rest of the page, so it cannot drift from the detail views.

King then asked for the summary to come first, for the scenario switch to be explained where it sits, and for plainer names for the three settings. The executive summary is now the landing tab; the switch reads "Fast 1.5× pace", "Central measured pace", "Slow 0.6× pace", with the multipliers read from the model; and a line under it says what each setting does to adoption, waiting times and barriers, and which to use for planning, stress tests and budgets. The summary's own figures follow the switch.

12. Colour and shape

King found the page read like a wall of text, and pointed at one symptom: the three scenario buttons looked the same, so nothing said they could be chosen. Claude proposed a six-point design pass and King approved it. Each scenario now has its own colour (orange for fast, teal for central, blue for slow) that runs through the button, a band along the top of the page, the "AI doing" line on every chart and the shaded fast-to-slow range, so a reader always knows which setting the numbers belong to; the chosen button is filled and ticked. Years in the tables sit on warm-to-cool shading, so early years stand out without reading every cell. Each evidence tier has a fixed colour across pills, bars and legends. The executive summary opens with the economy's own chart and a small staircase of the evidence ladder; the three source cards carry an icon and coloured steps for what existed, what was added and why it matters. Long method passages fold behind headings the reader opens when needed, and the one-line summaries sit in tinted boxes.

The first build failed its own accessibility check twice: the orange was a shade too light for the unselected fast button, and in dark mode white text on the light fills was unreadable. Both were fixed by darkening the orange and using dark text on dark-mode fills. The screenshots then showed the ladder's labels overlapping, the summary saying "2031" while its chart said "2030 mid", and the phone header eating a third of the screen. The ladder was relaid with the conversion rates in the free space above each step, every year on the page now uses the same year-and-season wording, the scenario explanation folds away on phones and the tabs scroll in one row.

The three kinds of interaction, with examples

King worked with Claude as a tool

King named an outcome and Claude produced it without further discussion. Fourteen of the twenty-nine interactions were of this kind, and they carried most of the volume of work.

  • "please pull in the wages and employment data" (message 3) became tasks 14, 17 and 18: three tables found, 523 occupations coded to wages, 43 employment groups.
  • "can we try to get the downloads again?" (message 4) became task 17: three approaches tried, the third worked.
  • "do thorough QA" (message 6) became tasks 21 and 22: five kinds of check, nine fixes, zero remaining accessibility violations.
  • "Both. And can I search by tasks too?" (message 8) became tasks 25 and 26: alternative titles, typo tolerance and a task index of 59,000 entries, in one round.
  • "which a small language model are we using?" (message 10) became task 31: a plain explanation of the in-page model, its size, and its blind spots such as negation.

King and Claude worked as teammates

King asked a question or made a judgment, Claude answered with reasoning rather than output, and the exchange changed the plan. Five interactions, and they are the ones that shaped the work most.

  • "Wat do the honest limits mean?" (message 2). Claude's plain-language answer turned four caveats into the four-point agenda for the next two rounds.
  • "For 2 and 3, what do you suggest we do?" (message 3). Claude proposed a barrier index and a data-anchored adoption curve and built a first version; King later decided to park the discussion until 4 was done.
  • "Is 1 better now?" (message 4). Claude's answer was "half": a candid assessment King could not have got by reading the code.
  • "Let's come back to 2 and 3 after we do 4." (message 4). King set the order; Claude followed it.
  • "is it easier for the lay person if you create an artifact ... We can offer both options" (message 5). A question, an agreement, and a design decision: a searchable page rather than a fixed dashboard.
  • "When I search for an job, is it found by keywod matching" (message 7). King had used the page and probed exactly how it worked; the plain answer ("yes, and here is where that fails") produced the next round of work.
  • "can it matching be done using gen ai matching?" (message 8). An open question about what was feasible; the answer was two kinds of AI matching, one that runs inside the page and one that asks Claude, and both were built.
  • "how useful are these given how fast AI is mving?" (message 11) was the sharpest exchange of the whole project. It did not ask for a feature; it questioned the evidence behind the plan. The answer changed three things at once: old ratings became flags rather than verdicts, the adoption series became a live feed with a date on the page, and a fresh rating was added with its own reliability test. "Go ahead ... in the sequence you assess is the most robust and rigorous" (message 12) then handed Claude the ordering, which is a teammate move in the other direction: trust extended on the strength of the previous answer.

Each worked independently

Most of Claude's time was spent alone: six stretches, each ending in a pushed commit and a summary. Examples of decisions made without asking:

  • Using O*NET 30.1 as the spine and mapping 31.0's renumbered ids back by name (task 4), because every AI dataset still uses the old ids.
  • Combining two routes from O*NET to ISCO-08 and recording where they agree (task 5).
  • Throwing away the first timing model when it said every desk task was capable now, and recalibrating on observed delegation (task 9).
  • Trying a different spelling of a file name after two cleverer approaches failed (task 17).
  • Choosing a 4-million-parameter embedding model for in-page meaning search, after sizing the 2-million and 8-million alternatives against the page's download budget (task 27).
  • Round three was the longest independent stretch so far: tasks 34 to 38, about two hours of building, rating and testing between message 12 and the report back, with no exchange in between.

King's independent stretches sit between the messages: reading the report after round one, the limits explanation, the second-round summary, the page itself. They are inferred from what each next message picked up on, for example message 4 referring to "1" and "4" by number from the earlier list.

What the pattern says

By count, Claude executed 50 of the 65 tasks and worked alone for most of the elapsed time. By influence, the fifteen teammate exchanges mattered most: every one redirected the work, and four of them ("is 1 better now?", "offer both options", "is it found by keyword matching", "how useful are these given how fast AI is moving?") improved the honesty or reach of the result in ways the tool-style requests did not. A pattern worth naming: King's sharpest questions came after he had used the output himself. The tool-style requests were efficient because they were specific and came with a yes-or-no test of completion, such as whether the download succeeded. The open items King chose to defer, the model weaknesses 2 and 3, are still waiting for the teammate conversation he asked to return to.
