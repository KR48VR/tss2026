Where AI is doing Singapore's work, and when the rest could follow

This page joins the US O*NET database of jobs and tasks to Singapore's SSOC occupation codes and the SkillsFuture Skills Framework, then lays real AI-usage evidence on top: which tasks people already hand to AI, which they only get help with, and which stay physical or in person. A timing model turns that into a year by which half of a job's work could be done by AI and adopted by typical employers. Every number is a planning signal with a range, not a prediction of job loss.

What we built on, and what we added

O*NET, the US map of work

What exists

The US Department of Labor describes occupations as tasks, with the activities and skills behind each. It is the most detailed public map of work there is. It is American, and it says nothing about AI.

What we added
  • Linked every occupation to Singapore's own codes through official correspondence tables ( of SSOC codes), then to the Skills Framework.
  • Put evidence on every task: seen in real AI use, rated doable by human raters in 2023 and by Claude in 2026, raters disagree, unproven, or physical.
  • Gave every task a measured share of the work AI does when it is used, so "involved" and "doing" are kept apart.
  • A timing model with fast, central and slow cases, driven by measured adoption rather than assumed lags.
Why it matters

Singapore gets a task-level map it never had, and "exposure" becomes something you can trace task by task instead of a single score per job.

Singapore's own data

What exists

SingStat's occupation codes, SkillsFuture's Skills Framework ( roles and their key tasks), MOM's wages, employment, vacancies and retrenchments, and IMDA's adoption surveys. Rich and official, but published separately and never joined to each other or to AI evidence.

What we added
  • Joined them into one map: roles carry SingStat's own occupation code, the rest are matched by description with the match score shown.
  • Weighted everything by resident employment and by the wage bill, so the headline is about Singapore's pay, not US proxies.
  • Measured Singapore firms' adoption lead over the US from IMDA's surveys ( years), and gave each industry its own adoption date.
  • Added what has actually happened to each job since 2023 (vacancies, wages, employment, retrenchments) and test the map against it on every rebuild.
Why it matters

Numbers for Singapore's workforce, and a built-in reality check that will show whether the forecast is right as the years come in.

United States data

What exists

The Bureau of Labor Statistics' employment and wages for every occupation and industry, the Census Bureau's survey of firms using AI, O*NET's own career clusters, and the Department of Labor's industry competency models. Official and detailed, but never joined to each other or to AI evidence, and with no single equivalent of Singapore's Skills Framework.

What we added
  • Weighted every task by US employment and by the US wage bill ( million employed), so the headline is about US pay.
  • Gave each occupation its own adoption date from the industries its jobs sit in, measured from the Census sector series; the US sits on the adoption curve itself, with no lead.
  • Built a stand-in for the Skills Framework from three pieces: the 14 career clusters as sectors, the Department of Labor's competency model beside each cluster, and O*NET's skill levels with written examples on every occupation page.
  • Tested the map against what happened to each occupation's employment and pay between May 2023 and May 2024.
Why it matters

The same map for the US workforce, on the same footing as Singapore's, so the two can be compared and a third country added the same way.

Reports from AI and tech companies

What exists

Anthropic's usage data by task, Microsoft's Copilot study, OpenAI's ratings and usage shares, Google's lists, METR's capability trend, the US Census adoption survey. Each covers one company's users, one unit, one date. They cannot be added up, and most headlines come from the weakest kind of evidence, a rating.

What we added
  • Put all of them on the same task map, in the same units.
  • Refresh the adoption survey live; a frozen copy had been running a year behind reality.
  • Graded every source on a five-rung ladder, from "rated doable" to "the job changed", with its date and its measured track record: the 2023 ratings scored at telling which tasks would be seen in use (0.50 is a coin toss), and our 2026 rating agrees with human raters as well as they agree with each other.
  • A translator that turns any headline number into what it has historically meant further down the ladder.
Why it matters

One view instead of a dozen incompatible reports, and a way to size the next headline before reacting to it.

How far to trust it
    What to use it for
    • Workforce planning, job by job. Open any job and see which tasks AI already does, helps with, or cannot touch yet, and the year by which a quarter of the work could be AI's. The "AI helping" tasks stay with people but change; that is where reskilling pays.
    • Sector and skills comparisons. The Sectors tab ranks the Skills Framework sectorsthe 14 career clusters, each with the industry's competency model; the Skills tab shows which skills sit in the most exposed jobs and whether AI reaches low-level or high-level work first.
    • Reading the next report. Place it on the ladder and run its number through the translator before reacting.
    • Stress tests and budgets. The scenario switch at the top changes every number: use the fast case to stress-test, the slow case to budget.
    What it is not

    Not a prediction of job losses: a job whose tasks are disruptable changes, it does not disappear, and nothing here measures a single firm. Not a verdict on capability: "unproven" means nobody has shown it yet. On today's patterns AI does under half of almost any job even when every task is disrupted, which is why the headline year is "a quarter". The 2026 rating is a model rating its own kind; its agreement with human raters and a repeat run are the guards, and the usage data are allowed to overrule it.

    What to be careful about

    The two workforces are measured by different surveys in different years: Singapore's employment is residents in June 2025 and wages are monthly medians from June 2025; the US figures are all jobs in May 2024 with annual medians. Shares of each country's own wage bill are comparable; absolute wages are not, and are not compared. The US rung-five test uses one year of employment change (May 2023 to May 2024), Singapore's uses vacancies, wages and employment to 2025. Occupations that O*NET lists but the BLS does not publish separately (about twenty, mostly military) carry no US weight.

      Skill levels and AI

      Does AI reach low-level or high-level work first?

      O*NET rates, for every occupation, the level each skill is needed at on a 0 to 7 scale with written examples (Mathematics level 2: count the change for a customer; level 4: work out the floor area of a new house; level 6: build a mathematical model of an engineering problem). Occupations are grouped by the level a skill needs and compared, weighted by US employment: how much of their work is already seen in AI use, how much AI could be doing by 2035, and when a quarter is done. The pattern is about occupations, so it applies to the same jobs in Singapore with different weights. The levels describe what each job demands today, as rated by job holders and analysts, not what it will demand once AI is in use.

      What to be careful about

      The levels describe what a job demands today, rated by job holders and analysts, not what it will demand after AI. The level is per occupation, not per task, so this is a statistical reading across occupations rather than a task-by-task one. "Seen in AI use" reflects who uses AI today, office and software work first. The three bands are cut at levels 2.5 and 4.5; a band with few occupations is marked.

      O*NET skills and knowledge areas

      Which skills sit in the most exposed jobs

      Each skill is scored through the jobs that need it (importance 3 or more out of 5), weighted by how important it is in each. A skill that only appears in exposed jobs is not itself "automated"; it tells you where retraining demand will come from first.

        Source scorecard

        The reports behind this map, and how they have done so far

        Track record means a test against what was later observed, on this map's own data. A score of 0.50 means a rating told us nothing about which tasks would be seen in use; 1.00 would be perfect. Most honest ratings land near 0.6: real signal, far from certainty.

        Sources, what they measure and their track record

        Two rules that follow from the record: an old "AI can do this" has held up, an old "AI cannot" has not (a quarter of the tasks rated impossible in 2023 had been seen in use by 2026); and a rating that needs AI to be wired into company systems tells you less than one about a plain chatbot, because companies are slower than the technology.

        How to read this page

        Labels for a task. AI doing it means the task shows up in AI usage data (any release since December 2024) and, when it does, the AI mostly completes it rather than assisting. AI helping means it shows up but people mostly use AI to help. Physical means the task is hands-on and the relevant question is robotics, not chatbots. For tasks nobody has yet been seen handing to AI, the page shows the strongest remaining evidence: Rated doable (the 2023 human and GPT-4 raters in OpenAI's study said AI could halve the time, and so does Claude's October 2026 rating of every task on the same scale), Newly rated (the 2023 raters said no, only the 2026 rating says yes), Raters disagree, and Unproven (every rater said no and nothing has been seen). "Unproven" never means "AI cannot"; it means nobody has shown it yet. Unproven, disputed and newly rated tasks wait a measured lag before they count as capable (, and years in the central case), measured from how far behind rated tasks each group is in actually being seen in use. A fresh "yes" from one model alone has so far been the weakest predictor of actual use, which is why it waits longest.

        "Involved" and "doing". Picture a job made of ten equal tasks. By 2031 the model says AI is capable of six of them and employers have adopted it. Counting whole tasks, AI is involved in 60% of the job. But look at how much of each task AI actually does when people use it: nearly all of two of them, half of two, a fifth of two. That adds up to about three tasks' worth, so AI is doing about 30% of the job. This page leads with "doing", because that is what people mean by disrupted. The share AI does when it is used comes from the usage data for tasks that have been seen (the share of use where AI completes the task rather than helps), and from the 2026 end-to-end rating for tasks that have not, calibrated on the seen ones (). Two consequences worth knowing: on today's patterns AI does roughly 40% to 60% of almost any task it is used on, so the ceiling for most jobs is under half even if every task were disrupted; and that is why the headline year is "AI doing a quarter of the work", with "AI involved in half" kept beside it. Three numbers are always given: fast, central, slow. The honest statement is the range.

        What drives the timing

        Adoption: a curve fitted to the US Census survey of firms using AI, refreshed from the Census on every rebuild (latest reading , of firms); half of US firms by in the central case, fast and slow. Singapore firms run about years ahead, measured by comparing IMDA's enterprise survey with the US series at matched adoption levels, so half of Singapore firms by . Each job's industries move its date: from the Census sector series, information and communications firms run about years ahead of the Singapore average and accommodation and food services about years behind; an occupation takes the employment mix of its industries, and a Skills Framework role takes its sector. Then the capability term: the evidence lags above, a delay for long tasks using the measured doubling of AI task length every days, and robot-readiness tiers for physical tasks. Then a barrier delay for work that is interpersonal, in person, carries accountability, or that people keep for themselves even with AI around. The fast and slow cases change the adoption speed, the lags and the barrier delay together.

        What has happened so far

        Every job page ends with rung five of the ladder, the job market itself: MOM's job vacancies by detailed occupation (September 2023, 2024 and 2025), median gross wages by occupation (June 2024 and June 2025), employed residents by occupation sub-group (every June since 2011), and retrenchments per 1,000 employees, vacancy rates and resignation rates by occupational group. The map's exposure signals are tested against these outcomes with rank correlations, reported on the "Reading the news" view. So far they show no clear relation at the occupation level, and at the group level the more exposed groups have grown faster, not slower. That is a baseline against which the next releases can be judged, not evidence for or against the forecast: the forecast's central years mostly start in 2029.

        Where the numbers come from

        Anthropic's Economic Index (seven snapshots, December 2024 to May 2026) is the only source that publishes usage by task. Microsoft's Copilot study gives a second opinion at the activity level. OpenAI's 2023 study rated every task twice (human raters and GPT-4); Claude rated every task again in October 2026 on the same scale, through a re-runnable command in the open-source pipeline, and agrees with each 2023 rater about as often as they agreed with each other. OpenAI and Google also publish occupation-level ratings and lists. Firm adoption comes from the US Census Business Trends and Outlook Survey (national and by sector) and IMDA's Singapore Digital Economy Reports. Singapore employment is from MOM's Labour Force in Singapore 2025 and wages from MOM's Occupational Wage Survey, June 2025.

        What to be careful about

        The usage data reflect who uses Claude, which skews to office and software work, so "unproven" is weaker evidence for hands-on and frontline jobs. The Census reworded its question in late 2025 and the level jumped; the curve carries a step for it, and the old-wording curve would put the US half-way point at . The 2023 ratings are old: a "yes" from then still stands, a "no" is only a flag to look again, which is what the 2026 rating and the usage data do. Within each 2-digit occupation group, employment is split evenly across surveyed occupations because no finer official count exists; group totals are solid, single-occupation wage figures are not. Links between Singapore roles and O*NET are official where SingStat coded them and text-matched elsewhere; each match shows its score.

        Two countries, one model

        The task-level model is the same everywhere: what AI can do, how much of each task it does, the barriers and the evidence lags. Three things change with the country switch at the top: who does which jobs and what they are paid (MOM for Singapore; the Bureau of Labor Statistics for the US), where the country sits on the adoption curve (the US is the Census curve itself; Singapore runs a measured lead ahead of it), and which industries each occupation sits in. Singapore's Skills Framework has no US twin, so the US side uses O*NET's 14 career clusters as sectors, the Department of Labor's industry competency models for what each industry needs, and O*NET's skill levels per occupation in place of the Framework's proficiency levels. The "Singapore vs US" tab compares the two on one footing; shares of each country's own wage bill are comparable, absolute wages are not.

        Reading the years

        Tables and job pages show a year with a season, such as "2031 mid": early is the first third of the year, mid the middle third, late the last. That is as precise as the model deserves; many jobs sit within a year of each other, so the season is what separates them. Tables still sort by the exact value. The headline tiles round to the year.
