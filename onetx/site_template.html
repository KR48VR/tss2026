Where AI is doing Singapore's work, and when the rest could follow

This page joins the US O*NET database of jobs and tasks to Singapore's SSOC occupation codes and the SkillsFuture Skills Framework, then lays real AI-usage evidence on top: which tasks people already hand to AI, which they only get help with, and which stay physical or in person. A timing model turns that into a year by which half of a job's work could be done by AI and adopted by typical employers. Every number is a planning signal with a range, not a prediction of job loss.

This page has measured, on 18,796 real tasks, how often a claim on one rung has so far turned into the next.       Track record means a test against what was later observed, on this map's own data. A score of 0.50 means a rating told us nothing about which tasks would be seen in use; 1.00 would be perfect. Most honest ratings land near 0.6: real signal, far from certainty.

      Sources, what they measure and their track record

      Two rules that follow from the record: an old "AI can do this" has held up, an old "AI cannot" has not (a quarter of the tasks rated impossible in 2023 had been seen in use by 2026); and a rating that needs AI to be wired into company systems tells you less than one about a plain chatbot, because companies are slower than the technology.

      How to read this page

      Labels for a task. AI doing it means the task shows up in AI usage data (any release since December 2024) and, when it does, the AI mostly completes it rather than assisting. AI helping means it shows up but people mostly use AI to help. Physical means the task is hands-on and the relevant question is robotics, not chatbots. For tasks nobody has yet been seen handing to AI, the page shows the strongest remaining evidence: Rated doable (the 2023 human and GPT-4 raters in OpenAI's study said AI could halve the time, and so does Claude's October 2026 rating of every task on the same scale), Newly rated (the 2023 raters said no, only the 2026 rating says yes), Raters disagree, and Unproven (every rater said no and nothing has been seen). "Unproven" never means "AI cannot"; it means nobody has shown it yet. Unproven, disputed and newly rated tasks wait a measured lag before they count as capable (, and years in the central case), measured from how far behind rated tasks each group is in actually being seen in use. A fresh "yes" from one model alone has so far been the weakest predictor of actual use, which is why it waits longest.

      "Half-way year". For a job, the year by which half of its task weight is "disruptable": AI can do the task at workplace reliability and typical organisations have had time to adopt it. Three numbers are always given: fast, central, slow. The honest statement is the range.

      What drives the timing. Adoption: a curve fitted to the US Census survey of firms using AI, refreshed from the Census on every rebuild (latest reading , of firms); half of US firms by in the central case, fast and slow. Singapore firms run about years ahead, measured by comparing IMDA's enterprise survey with the US series at matched adoption levels, so half of Singapore firms by . Each job's industries move its date: from the Census sector series, information and communications firms run about years ahead of the Singapore average and accommodation and food services about years behind; an occupation takes the employment mix of its industries, and a Skills Framework role takes its sector. Then the capability term: the evidence lags above, a delay for long tasks using the measured doubling of AI task length every days, and robot-readiness tiers for physical tasks. Then a barrier delay for work that is interpersonal, in person, carries accountability, or that people keep for themselves even with AI around. The fast and slow cases change the adoption speed, the lags and the barrier delay together.

      Where the numbers come from. Anthropic's Economic Index (seven snapshots, December 2024 to May 2026) is the only source that publishes usage by task. Microsoft's Copilot study gives a second opinion at the activity level. OpenAI's 2023 study rated every task twice (human raters and GPT-4); Claude rated every task again in October 2026 on the same scale, through a re-runnable command in the open-source pipeline, and agrees with each 2023 rater about as often as they agreed with each other. OpenAI and Google also publish occupation-level ratings and lists. Firm adoption comes from the US Census Business Trends and Outlook Survey (national and by sector) and IMDA's Singapore Digital Economy Reports. Singapore employment is from MOM's Labour Force in Singapore 2025 and wages from MOM's Occupational Wage Survey, June 2025.

      What to be careful about. The usage data reflect who uses Claude, which skews to office and software work, so "unproven" is weaker evidence for hands-on and frontline jobs. The Census reworded its question in late 2025 and the level jumped; the curve carries a step for it, and the old-wording curve would put the US half-way point at . The 2023 ratings are old: a "yes" from then still stands, a "no" is only a flag to look again, which is what the 2026 rating and the usage data do. Within each 2-digit occupation group, employment is split evenly across surveyed occupations because no finer official count exists; group totals are solid, single-occupation wage figures are not. Links between Singapore roles and O*NET are official where SingStat coded them and text-matched elsewhere; each match shows its score.

      Reading the years. Tables and job pages show a year with a season, such as "2031 mid": early is the first third of the year, mid the middle third, late the last. That is as precise as the model deserves; many jobs sit within a year of each other, so the season is what separates them. Tables still sort by the exact value. The headline tiles round to the year.
