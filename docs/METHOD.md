# Method: frontier scores and the timing model

## 1. Frontier scores (what is AI doing today?)

Per O*NET task (`frontier_tasks`):

| Column | Meaning |
|---|---|
| `usage_latest` | share (%) of Claude.ai conversations mapped to the task, mean of the task's two most recent releases with published usage (`usage_last_vintage` says which). The 2026 monthly releases list about 1,100 tasks against 3,600 in the first release, so taking only the newest releases would turn thousands of tasks that were seen in use into "not observed". 0 means never published (below Anthropic's privacy threshold or unused) |
| `usage_index` | percentile rank of `usage_latest` among tasks with any usage; 0 for the rest |
| `usage_trend` | yearly change in log usage share across the vintages (needs ≥ 3 points). Positive = the task is gaining share of AI use |
| `automation_share` | among classified conversations, the share with an automation-style pattern (directive or feedback loop) as opposed to augmentation (validation, task iteration, learning). Imputed from the task's DWA, then IWA, then the global mean when unpublished (`automation_imputed`) |
| `ai_autonomy` | 1–5 mean autonomy rating (Jan 2026 release onwards) |
| `human_only_hours` | Anthropic's estimate of the time a person would need without AI |
| `human_only_ability_pct` | share of conversations where a human could have done the task alone |
| `penetration` | Anthropic labour-market "task penetration" |
| `ms_share`, `ms_completion`, `ms_scope` | Microsoft Copilot activity share, completion rate and impact scope, averaged over the task's IWAs |
| `physical`, `robot_tier` | from the robot-exposure release (E0–E3) |
| `observed_automation` | `1{usage_latest > 0} × automation_share` — Project Iceberg's "observed automation" signal |
| `status` | `physical_robotics`, `observed_automation` (seen in any release and automation_share ≥ 0.5), `observed_augmentation` (seen), `not_observed` (never published) |

Roll-ups are weighted means: tasks → activities and occupations use `task_weight` (importance share within the occupation); occupations → SSOC use the crosswalk's `weight_rev`; SSOC → Skills Framework roles use the role's SSOC weights; key tasks use their top-3 matched O*NET tasks weighted by match score. Skills get an importance-weighted mean over the occupations that need them (importance ≥ 3) and, separately, the mean over the GWAs O*NET links them to.

Per occupation (`frontier_occupations`) the other providers are joined: Microsoft's `ai_applicability_score`, OpenAI's 2023 `openai_exposure_beta_gpt4` / `_human` (Eloundou et al.), Google ATLAS's list membership (`google_atlas_flag`), Anthropic's `aei_job_exposure`, GDPval coverage and the robot index. `n_providers_positive` counts how many of Anthropic (≥ 25% of task weight observed), Microsoft (top third of applicability), OpenAI (beta ≥ 0.5) and Google (listed as over-represented or saturated) point the same way.

Sanity check: our task-weighted `observed_automation` per occupation correlates 0.67 with Anthropic's own occupation-level `observed_exposure` and 0.49 with Microsoft's AI applicability score (different data, different method), which is the kind of agreement one would hope for and no more.

## 2. Timing model (when could it be disrupted?)

Three components per task, each anchored on published data, each with a fast and a slow case.

### 2a. Capability — when could AI do it?

*Non-physical tasks.* AI is already used to do tasks of a few hours. We calibrate today's **delegation horizon** on the usage data: fit `automation_share ~ sigmoid(a + b·log2 minutes)` on observed non-physical tasks and take the length at which the fitted share crosses 50% (about 4.3 hours in the current build; `delegation_horizon_now_min` in `build_stats.json`). The horizon grows at METR's measured frontier doubling time (our re-fit of Time Horizon 1.1: about 106 days since 2024; fast 85 days; slow 212 days). A task is capable when the horizon reaches its length. Lengths come from Anthropic's per-task estimate where published (5,180 tasks), else the DWA / IWA / GWA median, else the Job Zone median (`minutes_source`). A task already observed with AI mostly doing it is capable now.

*Evidence tiers and the evidence lag.* Task length alone says "capable now" for almost every desk task, because most tasks are shorter than the delegation horizon. So a desk task is only treated as capable today when there is evidence for it. Each task gets an evidence tier (`frontier_task_evidence`, `forecast.evidence_tiers`): **seen** (AI use on the task has been published in at least one Economic Index release), **rated** (not seen, but rated doable, i.e. E1 or E2, by the 2023 raters in OpenAI's "GPTs are GPTs" study, human or GPT-4, and by Claude's October 2026 rating), **newly_rated** (the 2023 raters both said no, the 2026 rating says yes), **mixed** (the raters disagree) and **unproven** (every rater said no); physical tasks follow the robot path. A task that is not seen waits a lag before it counts as capable. The lag is measured, not assumed (`forecast.calibrate_evidence`): for desk tasks, the share of each rating group ever seen in use is tracked release by release (seven releases, December 2024 to May 2026); the rated group's coverage grows at about 8 percentage points a year, and a group whose coverage today equals what the rated group had k years ago lags by k years. Current central lags: mixed 1.0 years, unproven 1.9 years, newly rated 3.1 years (fast = half, slow = double). The newly-rated lag is the longest: a fresh "yes" from one model against both 2023 raters has so far been the weakest predictor of actual use. "Unproven" never means "AI cannot".

*The 2026 rating.* `python -m onetx rate` has Claude (Sonnet, through the Claude Code command line, batches of 80 tasks, about 25 minutes and US$15) rate every O*NET task on `onetx/rubric_2026.md`: exposure on the 2023 E0/E1/E2 scale, end-to-end autonomy A0–A3 and the main barrier. Results are cached in `data/reference/ai_task_ratings_<version>.csv` and used once at least 90% of tasks are rated. Validation (October 2026 run): the 2026 yes/no agrees with the 2023 human raters on 80% of tasks (kappa 0.60) and with GPT-4 on 84% (kappa 0.69), while the two 2023 raters agreed with each other on 82% (kappa 0.64). 90% of robot-flagged tasks are rated with a physical barrier and 4.5% of non-physical tasks are. Among seen desk tasks the autonomy rating tracks the observed automation share (A3 tasks 59%, A0 tasks 41%; correlation 0.22), which the exposure rating does not. Against the 2023 raters, 26% of desk tasks both called "no" are now rated doable and 17% of tasks at least one called "yes" are now rated not. A repeat rating of 300 random tasks in fresh batches agreed with the first on 84% of exposure labels (kappa 0.73; 89% on yes/no), 78% of autonomy levels (99.7% within one level) and 77% of barrier reasons (kappa 0.71). Re-run with a new rubric version to measure how the frontier moves.

*Physical tasks.* The robot-exposure tier sets years-from-now to capability (fast / central / slow): E1 purpose-built cell 2 / 4 / 8; E2 structured human facility 4 / 7 / 12; E3 unstructured setting 8 / 12 / 20; E0 beyond the horizon.

### 2b. Adoption — when do organisations let it?

*US curve, refreshed every build.* The US Census Business Trends and Outlook Survey asks firms every two weeks whether they used AI in producing goods or services. `python -m onetx fetch` downloads the national and sector files from census.gov whenever the local copy is two weeks old, so the curve never again runs on a stale copy. Two wordings are spliced: the original "Artificial Intelligence (AI)" question (51 readings, Sept 2023 to Aug 2025, 3.7% to 9.7%, from the copy in the AEI release) and the reworded "Super Intelligence or SI (formerly referred to as Artificial Intelligence or AI)" question (Nov 2025 onward; 24 readings to 2026-10-04, 24.6%). The level jumped at the rewording (9.7% in Aug 2025 to 17.3% in Nov 2025, more than the whole prior two years), so the fit `logit(p/K) = a + b·t + c·[new wording]` carries a step: growth 0.506 a year on all 75 readings, step 0.57. The curve used runs at the new-wording level and crosses half of US firms in **2029.2** (central), 2027.5 with growth 1.5x faster, 2032.7 at 0.6x; the old-wording level would say 2030.3. The ceiling K is 90% by assumption. A stale copy fitted to Aug 2025 had predicted 15% of firms for Oct 2026; the Census reported 24.6%, which is why the feed is live.

*Singapore lead, measured from firms.* IMDA's annual survey of enterprises (`data/reference/singapore_ai_adoption_imda.csv`, hand-transcribed from the Singapore Digital Economy Reports with a source per row) gives the share of Singapore firms that used AI: 2024: 14.7%, 2025: 23.5% (all enterprises; SMEs 4.2% in 2023, 14.5% in 2024, 23.4% in 2025; large local enterprises 44.0%, 62.5%, 70.4%). For each point the date at which the fitted US curve reaches the same share is read off; Singapore's annual figure is dated at year end, the conservative choice because a firm that used AI at any time in the year counts while the Census asks about the last two weeks. The mean gap over [2024, 2025] is a **lead of 0.64 years**, so half of Singapore firms by about 2028.6 in the central case; since IMDA's question (any AI use in the year) is looser than the Census's, this is an upper bound and the page says so (see *Measurement or upper bound* below). Singapore's own three points fitted alone put the half-way point at 2027.2, earlier still, but that fit leans on a derived 2023 figure. The earlier method (individual usage ratio converted through the growth rate, about 1.1 years) remains as the fallback when no firm series is available (`geo_lead_years`).

*US comparators by firm size (`ai_signals.load_btos_size_adoption`, `btos_comparators`).* The Census also publishes the question by employment-size class (A: 1-4 employees ... G: 250 or more; `census/btos_empsize.xlsx`, refreshed with the national file), but only since the reworded questionnaire of November 2025, too short a series to fit a growth rate of its own. So three comparator curves are built: "all" is the national fit itself; "10plus" and "100plus" hold the national growth rate and wording step and take the level of the firm-weighted mean of bands C-G (10 or more employees) and F-G (100 or more), the weights being the Statistics of U.S. Businesses 2022 counts of employer firms per band (`data/reference/us_firms_by_size_susb2022.csv`; SUSB's 200-299 class is split half and half at the 250 boundary). In the current build the 10-plus curve sits about 0.18 years ahead of the all-firm curve (its latest reading 26.7% against 24.6%) and the 100-plus curve about 1.3 years ahead (38.6%): most firms with 10 or more employees have 10 to 49, and those adopt at nearly the all-firm rate. Each country's lead is read against the comparator matching the firms its own survey covers (`CountrySpec.survey_firms`): all firms for Canada, Australia and Singapore; 10 or more for the UK and every European office, whose ICT surveys cover enterprises with 10 or more persons employed; 100 or more for Japan. The all-firm lead is kept beside it (`lead_years_all_firms`).

*Measurement or upper bound (`CountrySpec.survey_question`, `countries.lead_basis`).* Matching the firm sizes removes only a small part of the gap between the European readings and the US curve; the rest is the question. The Census asks whether the business used AI in producing goods or services in the last two weeks. Canada's survey asks the same question over twelve months (`production`). Every other survey asks something looser: the ABS and IMDA whether the business used AI at all during the year, the ONS and the European offices which AI technologies the enterprise uses, from a list (`checklist`), Japan's MIC whether the enterprise has introduced IoT or AI systems (`iot_ai`). A looser question counts more firms as users at the same date, so a lead read from it can only overstate the country's position. The page therefore marks every lead from a non-production question as an upper bound ("at most"), prints the all-firm figure beside the size-matched one, and says in each country's record what the survey covers and asks. No correction is applied, because no survey asks both questions of the same firms.

*Sector shifts.* The Census publishes the same question by NAICS sector. Holding the national growth rate, each sector's level offset converts into years ahead of or behind the national curve (`ai_signals.fit_sector_levels`), and `sector_adoption.py` carries those shifts to Singapore: NAICS sectors map to the industry groups MOM uses in Labour Force Table D2; an SSOC occupation takes a blend of its major group's industry mix (Table D2, employed residents by occupation group and industry) and the industries of the Skills Framework roles tagged to it (half each where tags exist); an O*NET occupation takes the crosswalk-weighted mean over its SSOC codes; a Skills Framework role takes its sector's industry (cross-sector frameworks such as Human Resource stay national). Current shifts run from -2.1 years (information and communications) to +2.0 years (accommodation and food services); SSOC codes range -1.4 to +1.7. As a check, the US-derived ordering of industries agrees with IMDA's 2023 sector adoption rates (Spearman -0.93, where -1 is perfect agreement). Shifts are divided by the growth multiplier in the fast and slow cases. Roll-ups re-anchor task years to the entity's own shift (`forecast.reshift_years`), so the same O*NET task can carry a different year in a bank's role and a restaurant chain's role.

### 2c. Barriers — what else holds it back?

`frontier.task_barriers` scores every task on four things, each 0–1:

| component | what it measures | source |
|---|---|---|
| interpersonal | share of the task's detailed activities in caring, selling, negotiating, teaching, supervising, public-facing GWAs | O*NET task→DWA→GWA links |
| in_person | occupation percentile of physical proximity, face-to-face discussion, contact with others, dealing with the public | O*NET work context |
| accountability | occupation percentile of consequence of error, impact and frequency of decisions | O*NET work context |
| handover | how often people kept the task even with AI in the loop (human_only_ability), 50–100% → 0–1 | Anthropic usage data |

`barrier_index` is a weighted sum (0.35 / 0.25 / 0.25 / 0.15) and delays adoption by `barrier_index × max_barrier_years` (fast 2, central 4, slow 6 years). Physical tasks are handled by the robot path. `barrier_type` names the dominant component so a reader can see *why* a task is late.

### 2d. Putting it together

```
disrupt_year = adoption anchor − Singapore lead + industry shift / growth multiplier + max(0, capable year − today) + barrier delay − 1 year if already observed being automated

where, for a desk task that is not seen, capable year is at least today + the evidence lag of its tier
```

Per task: `capable_year_{fast,central,slow}`, `barrier_years_{fast,central,slow}`, `disrupt_year_{p10,p50,p90}`, `timing_bucket`. Per occupation / SSOC / role / activity / skill (`forecast_*`): `share_by_2026 … share_by_2040`, `share_beyond_horizon`, `share_already_observed`, mean `barrier_index`, and `half_year_{p10,p50,p90}` (first year at which half the weight is disruptable). Skills Framework roles are timed through their key tasks (top-1 matched O*NET task each).

### 2d-bis. "Involved" and "doing"

Counting whole tasks answers "by when could AI be involved in half of this job?". The page now leads with the stricter question, "by when could AI be doing half (or a quarter) of this job's work?", because that is what people mean by disrupted. Each task carries an `ai_share`: how much of it AI does once it is used. For seen tasks it is the observed automation share (the share of AI use on the task where the AI completes it rather than helps). For unseen tasks it is the mean observed share of seen desk tasks with the same 2026 end-to-end autonomy rating (`forecast.calibrate_ai_share`: A0 0.41 (n=249), A1 0.42 (n=1393), A2 0.51 (n=2458), A3 0.59 (n=420); fallback 0.49). The time-saved measure in the usage data was considered and rejected: it sits near 90% for almost every task and does not separate jobs. `disruption_curve` then reports, per group, `done_by_<year>` = Σ weight × ai_share × 1{year ≤ Y} / Σ weight (with p10/p90), `ceiling_ai_share` = Σ weight × ai_share / Σ weight (what AI would be doing if every task were disrupted), and `quarter_done_year_*` / `half_done_year_*`, the first years by which the done share reaches 25% and 50%; the economy and sector tables carry the same. Two facts follow from the data: AI does roughly 40% to 60% of almost any task it is used on, so the ceiling is under half for most jobs (economy-wide, wage-weighted, 48%), and "doing half" is reached by few jobs within the horizon. That is why the headline year is "AI doing a quarter of the work", with "AI involved in half" kept beside it; the timeline shows both lines and the ceiling. `ai_share` is a conversation-pattern measure, not a measure of hours: when AI "helps" it still does part of the work, so the doing line is a lower bound on AI's share of the work and the involved line an upper bound.

### 2e. Singapore, weighted by people and pay

`sg_ssoc2024_weights` gives every SSOC 2024 code a wage and an employment figure. Wages: MOM Occupational Wage Survey June 2025, which carries official SSOC codes for 523 occupations (no title matching needed); codes it lacks take the 2024 survey matched by title, then the 2-digit group median, then the major-group median income (`wage_source` says which). Employment: MOM Labour Force in Singapore 2025, Table D8, resident employment for 43 SSOC sub-major groups (2-digit), split evenly across the surveyed occupations within each group because no finer official count is published. `sg_economy_exposure` then reports observed automation and the share of work disruptable by year for the whole resident workforce, each major group and each sub-major group, weighted by employment and by wage value (employment × wage). Economy and group figures are robust to the even split; single-occupation wage values are not.

### 2e-bis. A second workforce: the United States (`us_labour.py`, `us_economy.py`)

The task-level model is country-free: capability evidence, the share AI does of each task, barriers and evidence lags are the same everywhere. What differs by country is (1) who does which jobs and what they are paid, (2) where the country sits on the adoption curve, and (3) which industries its occupations sit in. The US is built as a second workforce on exactly those three inputs:

1. **Weights.** BLS OEWS, the latest May edition on disk (`us_labour.OEWS_YEAR`: May 2024 in this build; the loaders switch to May 2025 as soon as `oesm25nat.zip` and `oesm25in4.zip` are in `data/raw/bls/`, which BLS does not let a cloud address download, see DATA_SOURCES): employment and annual median wage by detailed SOC 2018 occupation. O*NET-SOC 2019 codes are SOC 2018 codes plus a specialisation suffix, so each O*NET occupation takes its SOC code's figures; where several O*NET occupations share one SOC code, the employment is split evenly (the convention the Singapore side uses for codes that only carry a group total). Codes OEWS publishes only in combination get the remainder of their broad group with the group's wage; about twenty codes (mostly military) carry no US weight. Both countries are placed on the ISCO-08 major groups for group-level comparison: SSOC major groups follow ISCO; US occupations take the ISCO major group with the largest weight in the O*NET to ISCO crosswalk.
2. **Adoption.** The US sits on the Census curve itself: the lead is zero. Singapore's measured lead (2b) is what separates the two countries' anchors.
3. **Industry mix.** The Census sector shifts (3B) are carried to US occupations through OEWS's employment by occupation and NAICS sector, the US counterpart of the LFS Table D2 mix used for Singapore. Government is not surveyed by the Census and sits at the national level.

`forecast_tasks` is run a second time with the US shifts and no lead, rolled up to O*NET occupations and aggregated with the US weights through the same `economy_exposure` code as Singapore. The gap between the two countries' "AI doing" curves is split into a **job-mix** part and a **timing** part by running each workforce on the other's timing (Singapore's weights on US timing; US weights on Singapore timing); the two parts are the average of the two orderings and add up to the gap exactly, scenario by scenario (`us_economy.decomposition`). The US rung-five test is the one-year employment and median-wage change by occupation between the two latest OEWS editions on disk (May 2023 and May 2024 in this build) against exposure, read with the same rules as section 5. Numbers for the current build are in `build_stats.json` under `countries` and on the page's "Singapore vs US" view.

**The country switch.** The page carries both countries: a switch at the top sets which workforce every tab shows (summary, overview, job search and pages, sectors), while the task-level views (skills, reading the news, how to read this) are shared, and the "Singapore vs US" tab compares the two. A third country needs the same three inputs.

**A stand-in for the Skills Framework.** Singapore's Skills Framework pairs each sector with roles, key tasks, and skills with proficiency levels. The US has no single equivalent, so three pieces stand in for it: (1) O*NET's 14 career clusters (National Career Clusters Framework; collected from O*NET OnLine, 997 of 1,016 occupations, 186 in more than one cluster) as the sector lens, aggregated with BLS employment weights through the same `economy_exposure` code (`us_economy.cluster_view`); (2) the Department of Labor's Competency Model Clearinghouse (30 industry models and the Building Blocks model; 1,020 competency blocks, 5,792 key behaviours; parsed from the published database scripts by `us_labour.load_competency_models`), matched to clusters by a hand-made crosswalk (`data/reference/cmc_models_to_career_clusters.csv`) and shown beside each cluster's occupations with their tier (industry-wide and industry-sector technical competencies; the occupation tiers are left to O*NET); (3) O*NET's own task statements (core and supplemental, with importance), grouped by work activity as the counterpart of critical work functions, and its skill and knowledge ratings with the level needed on the 0 to 7 scale, shown with the written example at the nearest anchor (`onet.load_level_anchors`) in place of proficiency levels.

**Skill levels against AI (`us_economy.skill_level_exposure`).** For each of the 10 essential and 25 transferable skills, occupations are banded by the level the job needs (below 2.5; 2.5 to 4.5; 4.5 and above) and compared, employment-weighted: the share of work seen in AI use, AI doing by 2035, and the quarter-done year read off each band's curve; plus the rank correlation of level with "AI doing by 2035" and with "seen in use" across occupations, and the same for the average level over the essential skills. The reading that comes out: work needing a high level of desk skills (reading, writing, speaking, critical thinking, programming) is seen in AI use far more than work needing little of them (correlations around +0.5 to +0.7), while hands-on skills (repairing, equipment maintenance, operation and control) run the other way (around -0.5); by 2035 the most AI-done work sits in the middle band for the essential-skill average, because the highest-level work carries more of the barriers that slow adoption. The levels are per occupation, not per task, and describe today's job demands.

Caveats: the two workforces are measured by different surveys in different years (residents in June 2025 against all US jobs in the latest OEWS May edition); shares of each country's own wage bill are comparable, absolute wages are not and are never compared; the US outcome test spans one year of a strong labour market.

### 2e-ter. Further workforces: Australia, the United Kingdom, Canada, New Zealand, Norway, Sweden, Finland, Denmark, the Netherlands, Ireland, Japan, Korea, Germany and France (`countries.py`, `au_labour.py`, `uk_labour.py`, `ca_labour.py`, `nz_labour.py`, `no_labour.py`, `eu_labour.py`, `jp_labour.py`, `kr_labour.py`, `de_labour.py`, `fr_labour.py`)

The US showed that a country needs three inputs. Australia, the UK and Canada are built through one shared layer (`countries.py`) that takes those inputs from a short country record (`CountrySpec`) and runs the same steps for each, so a further country is a record and a loader module, not new code paths:

1. **Crosswalk.** O*NET occupations reach the national classification through ISCO-08: O*NET → ISCO (the existing two-route crosswalk) composed with ISCO → ANZSCO (the ABS correspondence table, 6-digit occupations rolled up to the 4-digit unit group, equal weight per ISCO code) or ISCO → SOC 2020 (the ONS coding index, which gives an ISCO-08 code for each of its 32,670 job titles, so a SOC–ISCO pair is weighted by the number of titles that carry it). As for Singapore, `weight` sums to 1 per O*NET occupation and `weight_rev` to 1 per national code. The labour force cube uses ANZSCO 2013 v1.2 and the earnings and correspondence tables ANZSCO 2022; the two agree on all but 18 unit groups, and a 2022 code the cube does not carry is mapped to the carried codes of its minor group.
2. **Weights.** Australia: employed persons per unit group, the 2025 average of the four quarterly readings, with the "not further defined" residual codes shared out over the unit groups under the same prefix in proportion to their own employment; average weekly total cash earnings from the May 2025 earnings survey, a group mean where a unit group is too small to publish. UK: employee jobs and median gross annual pay per SOC 2020 unit group from the latest ASHE table 14 on disk (`uk_labour.ASHE_YEAR`: 2026 provisional, published October 2026), the mean where the median is suppressed, a group median where both are. Wage value = employment × pay, in each country's own currency and period; shares of a country's own wage bill are comparable across countries, absolute pay is not.
3. **Lead.** Each country's own firm survey is read against the fitted US curve the way IMDA's is for Singapore: for each national reading the date at which the US curve reaches the same share is read off, and the lead is the mean gap over readings dated 2024 or later. Australia has two readings (1.4% of businesses used AI in 2021-22, 12.1% in 2024-25; a financial year is dated at its end), so its lead rests on one generative-AI-era point. The UK has thirteen fortnightly readings of businesses currently using at least one AI technology (9.4% in October 2023 to 29.7% in October 2026; "not sure" is not counted as use), eleven of them from 2024. The lead feeds `ForecastParams.geo_lead_years_measured` exactly as Singapore's does. Since the comparability pass (2b, *US comparators by firm size*) each country is read against the US curve for firms of the sizes its survey covers, and a lead from a survey whose question is looser than the Census's is an upper bound. Measured in this build: Australian firms at most about 0.4 years behind all US firms (the ABS asks about any AI use in the year); Canadian firms about 0.5 years behind, the one fair measurement (the same question as the Census, over twelve months); UK firms at most about 0.65 years ahead of US firms with 10 or more employees (0.8 against all US firms); Norway at most 2.05, Sweden 2.3, Finland 2.4, Denmark 3.0, the Netherlands 2.1 and Ireland 0.9 years ahead of US firms of the same sizes (each about 0.2 years more against all US firms); Japan at most 0.2 years ahead of US firms with 100 or more employees (1.5 against all US firms, the size match mattering most where the survey covers only large enterprises); Singapore at most 0.6 years ahead. Half of firms using AI by about 2029 early in the US in the central case, and no earlier than the year each upper-bound lead implies elsewhere.
4. **Industry shifts.** Each industry's survey share is compared with the national share at the same date in logit units (readings where the national share is under 3% are skipped: 0 to 1% readings are too small to carry a level), averaged over dates, and turned into years with the US growth rate, so a high-adoption industry sits a measured number of years ahead of the national curve. Australia's readings are by ANZSIC division (17 of 19 divisions are surveyed), the UK's by SIC section (14 sections; finance and public administration are not in the survey and sit at the national level). The shift reaches occupations through the country's occupation-by-industry mix: ANZSCO major groups by division (the finest the ABS publishes), SOC 2020 minor groups by section (Census 2021, England and Wales); every code in a group inherits its shift, and an O*NET occupation takes the `weight_rev`-weighted mean of its codes.
5. **Timing and views.** `forecast_tasks` runs once per country with its lead and shifts; the task forecast is saved (`forecast_tasks_au`, `forecast_tasks_uk`) so the job pages show national task lists on the country's own timing. Tasks roll up to national codes through the crosswalk (moved to the code's own industry shift), to the whole economy and to the national major and sub-major groups (`se.economy_exposure`), and across to the other countries' weights for the job-mix / timing split (`pair_decomposition`, the same construction as the Singapore–US one, now for every pair).
6. **Rung five.** Australia: employment change between the 2024 and 2025 averages per unit group, and earnings change between the May 2023 and May 2025 surveys. UK: jobs and median pay change between the two latest ASHE editions, the earlier year's revised tables against the latest provisional ones (2025 revised and 2026 provisional in this build). Rank correlations against exposure at the code level and the employment-weighted change by exposure tercile, as for the US.
7. **Skill levels.** Both classifications carry an official skill level per occupation (ANZSCO 1 to 5, where 1 is the highest; SOC 2020 1 to 4 by sub-major group), so each country gets an employment-weighted reading of exposure by national skill level beside the O*NET skill-level test, which runs on the country's O*NET-level weights.
8. **The official task lists, and the UK standards.** The ABS's "Tasks Include" list for every unit group (3,009 items over 340 unit groups; the 24 "not elsewhere classified" groups list occupations instead) and the ONS's task list for every SOC 2020 unit group (2,649 items over all 412) are matched to O*NET tasks with the same hybrid text matcher and occupation-neighbourhood bonus used for SSOC task bullets (`match.match_ssoc_tasks`): mean top-match score 0.46 for Australia and 0.44 for the UK, with 76% and 85% of top matches inside the occupations the code maps to. Each job page lists the official tasks with the matched task's label, year, 2026 rating and match score. For the UK, Skills England's 712 apprenticeship standards approved for delivery stand in for Skills Framework roles: a standard is assigned to SOC codes through its typical job titles looked up in the coding index (496 standards) or, where no title appears there, by matching its title and summary against each unit group's title, description and tasks (216); its 7,617 duties are matched to O*NET tasks like key tasks, and the 15 routes give a sector view weighted by the employee jobs of the codes each route leads to. A standard's level is a qualification level for the whole apprenticeship, not a proficiency level per duty, so, as in the US, the level a job needs comes from the occupation (SOC skill level, O*NET ratings) rather than the framework.

**Canada, the same steps with a direct link and a national O*NET.** Canada (`ca_labour.py`) runs through the same record, with three differences. (1) The crosswalk needs no ISCO step: Statistics Canada publishes a concordance from the US SOC 2018 to NOC 2016 and another from NOC 2016 to NOC 2021, so each O*NET-SOC 2019 occupation reaches its NOC 2021 unit group through its SOC 2018 code with equal weight over the NOC codes a SOC code splits into (1,956 pairs; all 516 unit groups and 1,016 O*NET occupations linked). The record's `onet_link` loader replaces `isco_link` wherever a country has such a concordance. (2) The weights are the 2021 Census: employed persons by NOC 2021 unit group (17.1 million) and the median employment income of full-year full-time workers in 2020 (511 unit groups; a minor-group median for the five where the income cell is suppressed), pulled from the Statistics Canada Web Data Service, which returns one cell per coordinate rather than a file (`fetch.fetch_statcan_api` reproduces the four pulls). The adoption series is the Canadian Survey on Business Conditions' question on using AI to produce goods or deliver services over the past twelve months: 6.1% of businesses in the second quarter of 2024, 12.2% in 2025 and 19.2% in 2026, by NAICS sector (17 of 20 sectors surveyed); the occupation-by-industry mix is the Census table of NOC minor groups by NAICS sector. Rung five uses the quarterly Job Vacancy and Wage Survey (vacancies per unit group, the four quarters to mid-2026 against the four before) and Job Bank's median wage per unit group (the 2024 and 2025 releases). The NOC's second digit is its TEER category (training, education, experience and responsibilities, 0 management to 5 no formal education), which is the national skill level. (3) OaSIS, the Occupational and Skills Information System from Employment and Social Development Canada, gives every unit group a lead statement, a list of main duties (4,965 over all 516 groups), example and index titles (18,654) and a rating for each of 35 skills, 42 work activities (0 to 5) and 33 knowledge areas (0 to 3), built on O*NET's descriptors. The duties are matched to O*NET tasks like the ABS and ONS lists (mean top-match score 0.54, higher than either, since the duties are written in O*NET's style; 59% of top matches inside the occupations the code maps to, lower, because a Canadian unit group often spans several SOC occupations). The ratings give the first national check on the O*NET skill-level test: `rating_level_exposure` bands the unit groups by the level OaSIS says each skill needs (0 to 1.5 basic, 1.5 to 3.5 working, 3.5 and up advanced) and reads exposure by band and the rank correlation of level against "AI doing by 2035", for each skill and for the average over all skills, with Canadian employment as the weight; the page shows it beside the O*NET reading for the same jobs, and each job page lists the top-rated skills, work activities and knowledge areas from OaSIS.

**New Zealand, on open data plus the Data Explorer (medium).** New Zealand (`nz_labour.py`) shares ANZSCO with Australia: the 2023 Census publishes employed people by unit group (358 codes, 2.62 million people; Stats NZ's own file, republished by Figure.NZ), and those codes coincide with the ones the Australian build carries, so the ABS correspondence table, task lists, title index and skill levels apply unchanged. Two inputs come from the Aotearoa Data Explorer API with a registered key (`fetch.fetch_statsnz_ade`, `STATSNZ_API_KEY`; each pull is keyed to the 2023 Census and the New Zealand total, since the unkeyed table runs over 2,500 areas and times out). (1) Pay: the Explorer publishes Census income by occupation down to the sub-major group (two digits, table CEN23_WRK_011, with a median row per group) and no unit-group income table, so every unit group carries its sub-major group's median total personal income for 2023, divided by 52 (43 figures in place of the eight major-group medians from the Household Labour Force Survey, which remain the fallback and the pay-change test). (2) Industries: the Explorer's occupation-by-industry table (CEN23_WRK_008) gives the eight major groups by the 19 ANZSIC divisions, the same resolution as Australia's mix. What New Zealand still lacks is a firm survey: no New Zealand survey has asked firms about AI use (Stats NZ's first Survey of Business Operations with that question reports later in 2026), so the record names Australia as its `lead_proxy`: Australia's measured lead stands in, and, because both countries' surveys and Censuses use the ANZSIC divisions, Australia's industry shifts are carried through New Zealand's own Census mix (`build_country` marks this as `industry_shifts_proxy`); the page says so on the leaders card, the lead sentence and the Sectors tab, and shows no readings as New Zealand's. Rung five uses the 2018-to-2023 Census employment change per unit group (Stats NZ changed its occupation coding in 2023, so small changes mean little) and the major group's earnings change, June 2025 to June 2026. The record is marked medium: pay is a group median and the adoption inputs are borrowed.

**Norway, on ISCO itself.** Norway (`no_labour.py`) is the first European record. Statistics Norway's register-based employment and earnings statistics (table 11658, through the open data.ssb.no API) give employees, jobs and median monthly earnings by four-digit STYRK-08 occupation every quarter; STYRK-08 uses ISCO-08's own codes, so O*NET reaches the 400 occupations with employees (2.84 million employees, second quarter of 2026) through its ISCO crosswalk alone, with no national correspondence table. The median of the minor group stands in for the 39 occupations whose four-digit earnings cell is suppressed. The adoption series is the ICT usage in enterprises survey (tables 13265 and 14942): the share of enterprises with 10 or more employed using at least one AI technology, 11% in 2021, 9% in 2023, 21% in 2024, 30% in 2025 and 48% in 2026, by eight industry groupings, the longest firm-survey series on the map; a survey year is dated at its second quarter. The occupation-by-industry mix is the EU Labour Force Survey's ISCO major group by NACE section table for Norway (Eurostat `lfsa_eisn2`, 2024), with sections keyed to the survey's groupings. Rung five is the one-year change in employees and median earnings per occupation from the same register table (second quarter of 2025 to 2026), the most current outcome test of any country here. Norway publishes no task list per occupation, so its job pages carry the O*NET occupations the code maps to and their timing, without a national task list; ISCO's four skill levels are the national skill level.

**Five more offices through their open APIs (`eu_labour.py`), and a depth mark on every record.** Sweden, Finland, Denmark, the Netherlands and Ireland are built from one module, each office answering one or two API queries (`fetch.fetch_further_offices`). Every country record now carries a `depth` (full, medium or limited) and a one-sentence `depth_note` on what is coarse or borrowed; the page shows both at the top of the overview and on the leaders card, and the method note lists them. *Sweden (full)*: Statistics Sweden's occupational register (employees by four-digit SSYK 2012, all sectors, 2024), its salary structure statistics (median monthly salary by the same codes, 2025; a minor- or sub-major-group median for small codes), and its ICT-use survey (enterprises with 10 or more employees using at least one AI technology, 2021 to 2025, by SNI industry ranges keyed to NACE sections). SSYK 2012 is built on ISCO-08 but renumbers below the minor group, so the link to O*NET runs through SCB's own translation key (appendix 2 of the SSYK 2012 standard, parsed from the PDF): each ISCO minor group names the SSYK classes it maps to, a four-digit SSYK code equal to the ISCO code takes the whole weight, and otherwise the minor group's SSYK occupations share it equally. *Finland (full)*: register-based employment statistics (employed by occupation, four-digit level, 2024; the classification carries ISCO-08's own codes, so the link is direct), the structure of earnings (median hourly earnings by four-digit occupation, 2024, published for one year so far, so no pay-change test), and the ICT-use survey (AI, 2021 to 2025, by industry). *Denmark (medium)*: earnings per hour worked by four-digit DISCO-08 (2023 to 2025) and the ICT-use survey (AI, 2023 to 2026, by six industry groups), but the open API publishes register employment by industry rather than occupation, so each two-digit group's EU-LFS employment is split across its four-digit occupations in the proportions of Norway's register, the nearest country with those codes (equal shares where Norway has no code), and there is no employment-change test. *Netherlands (medium)*: the labour force survey's employed persons (2025) and median hourly wages of employees (latest year since 2022 per group) for the 114 BRC 2014 occupation groups, which sit between two and three ISCO digits; ROA and CBS publish the ISCO 2008 composition of every group with 2025 worker counts (appendix 2 of the 2025 edition, parsed from the PDF), which gives the link to O*NET and each group's skill level (that of its largest ISCO occupation); the ICT-use survey (AI, 2023 to 2025, by industry). *Ireland (medium)*: the Census 2022 labour force (persons at work or looking for work) by 329 detailed occupational groups on UK SOC 2010 codes, linked to O*NET through the ONS SOC 2010 to ISCO-08 mapping (one-to-one and one-to-two with 50:50 or 40:60 splits), pay only by major group (Eurostat SES, mapped from SOC to ISCO major groups), and the CSO's enterprise AI survey (2023 to 2025) published nationally only, so no industry shifts; the Census 2016 to 2022 change is the employment test. None of the five publishes a task list per occupation, so their job pages carry O*NET's tasks through the link, as Norway's do. Survey years are dated at their second quarter.

**Japan (medium, `jp_labour.py`).** Japan's statistics are open to download from e-Stat by file id without a key (`fetch.fetch_estat`), but no table joins employment, pay and a classification that maps to ISCO. The record uses the latest Basic Survey on Wage Structure on disk (`jp_labour.WAGE_YEAR`: the 2025 edition, released March 2026, with 2024 kept beside it), table 1 by occupation: 144 occupation classes with the number of regular workers they represent (enterprises with 10 or more employees, 28.9 million workers in 2025) and monthly scheduled earnings; table 20 gives the same workers by occupation major group and JSIC industry, the mix that carries industry shifts. The adoption series is the Communications Usage Trend Survey's enterprise question, asked every year since 2018, on whether the firm has introduced IoT or AI systems or services (enterprises with 100 or more employees; 12% in 2018, 27% in 2025), by eight industry groupings keyed to JSIC divisions; it names IoT and AI together, which the page says. Japan publishes no correspondence from its occupation classification to ISCO-08, so the link is a table written for this map (`JSOC_TO_ISCO` in the module): each of the 144 classes names one or several ISCO-08 unit groups, shared equally, with an English title; the class's skill level is the mean ISCO level of those groups. The page's search accepts the Japanese class names as alternative titles. Rung five is the change in workers represented and in monthly scheduled earnings per class between the two latest editions (June 2024 to June 2025: all 144 classes, median change -0.6% in workers and +3.2% in earnings), read with the same rules as the other countries; the Communications Usage Trend Survey had no 2026 edition on e-Stat as of October 2026.

**Korea (medium, `kr_labour.py`).** KOSIS, the national statistics portal, issues API keys only to members, and membership needs a Korean mobile phone (the office confirmed this in writing to a foreign user in October 2026), so the record is built from the website's own table downloads instead: `fetch.fetch_kosis` opens each table page in a headless browser, ticks the classification levels and periods in the query settings the way a visitor does, runs the search and saves the page's CSV with codes (`data/raw/kosis/<table>/`). The tables: the Local Area Labour Force Survey's employed persons by three-digit occupation (DT_1ES3B32J, 163 groups of the 2024 classification, both sexes, half-yearly, 28.97 million in the first half of 2026) and by industry section and occupation major group (DT_1ES3B33J, the mix); the Ministry of Employment and Labour's Survey on Labour Conditions by Employment Type (DT_118N_PAYM41: workers and mean monthly total wages by three-digit occupation, 2024 and 2025, still on the 2017 classification); and the Survey of Business Activities' question on fourth-industrial-revolution technologies (DT_1EP1237_1 for 2017 to 2022, DT_1EP1337_1 from 2023: enterprises developing or using each technology, artificial intelligence among them, by industry section and division). The link to O*NET runs through Statistics Korea's official tables (kssc.kostat.go.kr): the 8th revision's four-digit codes to ISCO-08 unit groups, rolled up to three digits (`data/reference/ksco8_isco08_link.csv`, 428 unit groups covered), and the 7th-to-8th revision link that carries the wage survey's pay to the new codes as a workers-weighted mean (`ksco8_ksco7_link.csv`); a code without a converted pay line takes its two-digit group's mean. The firm survey covers enterprises with 50 or more regular employees and capital of 300 million won or more and asks which technologies the enterprise develops or uses, so its share (1.4% in 2017, 4.5% in 2022, 6.4% in 2023, 9.6% in 2024, each dated at the end of its year) is read against a "50plus" comparator (bands E to G) and the lead is an upper bound. Employment change is the first half of 2025 to the first half of 2026; pay change 2024 to 2025. English titles come from the table's own classification tree; the Korean names are alternative titles for the search. No official task list.

**Germany (full, `de_labour.py`).** Germany is the first record built through a registered account: Destatis's GENESIS-Online REST interface takes the account's email and password as request headers (`DESTATIS_USER`, `DESTATIS_PASSWORD`; `fetch.fetch_genesis`), and the tables that are too big for one call (the earnings survey by occupation) are pulled one reference year at a time. Employment comes from the Federal Employment Agency's employment statistics, "Beschäftigte nach Berufen" (two editions, 30 June 2025 and 30 June 2024, downloaded as spreadsheets from statistik.arbeitsagentur.de): employees subject to social insurance by four-digit KldB 2010 occupation with the four requirement levels as columns, and since KldB 2010's fifth digit is that requirement level, the columns are the 1,294 five-digit occupations with employees (34.7 million; a cell the agency suppresses takes an equal share of what the row's total leaves). Pay is GENESIS table 62361-0030, the earnings survey's median gross monthly earnings of all employees by five-digit occupation, April 2025 (1,125 codes directly; 166 take their three-digit group's published median and 3 their two-digit group's). The link to O*NET is the agency's official key from the five-digit KldB 2010 (revised 2020) to ISCO-08 unit groups: a unique code gives its whole weight to its target, and where the key lists alternatives the main target counts twice and each alternative once; ISCO-to-KldB weights are those shares times employees. The adoption series is the ICT-use survey as Destatis publishes it (GENESIS 52911: 11% of enterprises with 10 or more persons employed used AI in 2021, 12% in 2023, 20% in 2024 and 26% in 2025, by WZ 2008 section; the 2025 reading sits in a second table after the questionnaire changed), the same survey Eurostat reports, so the lead is read against US firms with 10 or more employees and is an upper bound. No German table crosses KldB occupations with industry, so the mix is derived: each two-digit KldB group's ISCO-major composition (its codes' key targets, weighted by employees) times the EU Labour Force Survey's section distribution of each ISCO major group for Germany. The requirement level is the national skill level (1 helper and trainee activities to 4 highly complex activities). Rung five is the one-year change in employees per five-digit code (June 2024 to June 2025) and in the code's own median earnings (April 2024 to April 2025). Titles are the key's English ones, with the German titles as alternative titles for the search; no official task list. What is coarse: employees subject to social insurance only (no civil servants, self-employed or marginal jobs), and the derived mix.

**France (full, `fr_labour.py`).** France is built through INSEE's Melodi API (`fetch.fetch_melodi`), which answers without a key at thirty calls a minute (the portal's application key is for INSEE's other APIs, and Melodi refuses it as a header). Employment and pay come from the wage tables built on the Déclaration sociale nominative: the private sector's "Salaires par catégorie socioprofessionnelle détaillée" (DS_DERA_PRIVE_ANNUEL, 2024: employees in full-time equivalents and mean net monthly salary per FTE for 366 detailed PCS-ESE categories, four characters) and the civil service's (DS_DERA_PUBLIC_ANNUEL, 2023, 75 categories), summed per category with the pay as the FTE-weighted mean: 411 categories, 23.1 million full-time equivalents. The PCS-ESE codes are the PCS 2003 profession codes (plus seventeen 2017 variants), so the link to O*NET runs through two INSEE matrices composed: PCS 2003 to PCS 2020 and PCS 2020 to ISCO-08, both probabilistic ("ns" and "a" cells count as half a percent); a 2017 variant without a row takes the mean of the PCS 2003 professions with its three digits, and ISCO-to-PCS weights are those probabilities times FTE employees. The two matrices leave a long tail of links carrying a percent or two of an ISCO group (49,000 O*NET-to-PCS pairs before pruning, seven in ten below one percent of an O*NET occupation), so links under 2% of an ISCO group are dropped and the rest renormalised, every category keeping its strongest link; 96% of the weight stays and the crosswalk has 23,000 pairs, in line with the UK's and Germany's. The adoption series is Eurostat's survey for France (run by INSEE and also on Melodi as DS_TICE: 6.7% in 2021, 5.9% in 2023, 9.9% in 2024, 18.2% in 2025, by activity), read against US firms with 10 or more employees as an upper bound. The mix is the labour force survey's employed persons by PCS group and activity (DD_EEC_ANNUEL, 2025), published for the six one-digit groups by detailed activity, summed to NACE sections. The skill level is the mean ISCO level of the unit groups a category links to. Melodi carries one year of each wage table, so there is no outcome test yet; titles are French; no official task list. What is coarse: the weights count salaried employees in full-time equivalents (the self-employed, about a tenth of employment, are not in the wage tables), pay is a mean net salary rather than a median, and the link is probabilistic.

**Europe at a glance (`europe.py`).** Every lead in this table is read against the US curve for firms with 10 or more employees and is an upper bound, since Eurostat's question is the checklist one (see 2b). Eurostat publishes, for 32 countries at once, the enterprise AI-use survey (`isoc_eb_ai`, by country, and `isoc_eb_ain2`, by NACE activity: 2021, 2023, 2024, 2025), employment by two-digit ISCO-08 group (`lfsa_egai2d`, EU-LFS, 2025), mean hourly earnings by ISCO major group (`earn_ses22_14`, the 2022 Structure of Earnings Survey; 2018 where a country has not published 2022 by occupation) and occupation by economic activity (`lfsa_eisn2`). The "Europe: 32 countries" tab ranks the countries by the year AI could be doing a quarter of their wage bill, with each country's readings, lead, and sub-major groups and industries on request. **What is not there.** The Australian Skills Classification (Jobs and Skills Australia) could not be reached from the build environment, so Australia has no skills-framework analogue beyond ANZSCO's skill levels and task lists; it can be added as a loader when the files are available. The Australian lead rests on one point and a question about use in the year, and the ABS industry readings on one year; the UK Census mix is England and Wales only. Canada's Census employment and income are from May 2021 and the 2020 income year, older than the other countries' weights, because the Labour Force Survey does not publish employment at the unit-group level; its vacancy and wage outcomes are the most current of the five. Country stats (leads, shifts, tests) are written to `build_stats.json` under `countries.<code>` for every record (AUS, GBR, CAN, NZL, NOR, SWE, FIN, DNK, NLD, IRL, JPN, KOR, DEU, FRA). MOM's server refused every spreadsheet download from the build environment in October 2026 (HTTP 403 on each spelling), so the Singapore industry mix and outcome tables built in an earlier run are kept when the raw files are absent (`sector_adoption.build_shifts`, `build.build_forecasts`); the US adoption curve they depend on was unchanged.

### 2f. How to read it

* A half-way year of 2031 means: under the central assumptions, by 2031 AI could be involved in half of this job's task weight and organisations will have had time to adopt it; "AI doing a quarter" and "AI doing half" are the stricter years that count only the share of each task AI completes. p10/p90 give the fast and slow cases; the honest statement is the range.
* Jobs re-bundle. A role whose tasks are disruptable does not disappear; it changes. Pair these numbers with demand (vacancies, wages) before drawing conclusions.
* Regulation, liability and price are not modelled beyond the accountability barrier. Change the parameters if you know better.

## 3. Known gaps and next steps

1. **Usage data are still provider-skewed.** Anthropic is the only provider publishing task-level usage. Microsoft (activity level), OpenAI (occupation-level exposure from 2023, GWA shares from 2025) and Google (lists of over- and under-represented occupations, 2026) are second opinions, not substitutes; `n_providers_positive` shows where they agree. OpenAI's 2026 "Work at the Frontier" series and Google's ATLAS publish no downloadable task tables yet; wire them in when they do.
2. **Relative, not absolute, usage.** Usage shares sum to 100% within a vintage; a falling share can still be rising use. Treat `usage_trend` as where the frontier is moving, not as adoption.
3. **Barriers are proxies.** The barrier index uses O*NET's occupation-level work context and activity links; it cannot see regulation or liability for a specific task. Where you know better (a licensed act, a statutory sign-off), override `barrier_index` for those tasks.
4. **Adoption is still shaped by the US curve.** Its speed comes from the US series; Singapore's level comes from IMDA's annual points (three so far, one of them derived), and the industry shifts are US sector levels carried over. IMDA's sector rates agree on the ordering, not yet on the size. A longer Singapore series would let the speed be fitted here too.
5. **Employment detail.** Official resident employment stops at the 2-digit SSOC group (43 groups). Below that, the even split across surveyed occupations is the weakest link in the wage-value view; the Occupational Employment Dataset that would fix it is not published.
6. **Text matches are approximate.** Filter on `score` / `in_mapped_occ`; hand-check the roles that matter to you.
7. **Capability evidence is still indirect.** "Seen in use" reflects who uses Claude; the 2023 and 2026 ratings are judgements, not demonstrations; the evidence lags are measured on usage coverage, which grows partly because the sample grows. Real-world agent benchmarks (Remote Labor Index, GDPval win rates) would be a better anchor when task-level files are published. The repeat-rating check in the QA log gives the 2026 rating's own reliability.
8. **One model rating its own kind.** The 2026 rating is Claude's view of what AI can do. Its agreement with the 2023 human raters is the guard, and the measured lag for "newly rated" tasks shows the data are allowed to overrule it.

## 4. Track record and the ladder (`validation.py`, the page's "Reading the news" view)

Every claim about AI and work sits on one rung of a ladder: (1) someone rated the task doable; (2) people have been seen using AI on it; (3) when they do, AI mostly completes it; (4) typical employers have adopted it; (5) the job itself has changed. The map holds data for rungs 1 to 4 and models rung 4; rung 5 is not in it. `validation.rating_validation` scores every rating against what was later observed and measures the conversion between rungs. All figures are on desk (non-physical) tasks, 2023 ratings against usage to May 2026.

| Rating | Tells which tasks will be seen in use (AUC; 0.5 = nothing, 1 = perfect) | Tells, among seen tasks, where AI mostly does the work (AUC) |
|---|---|---|
| OpenAI 2023, human raters | 0.59 | 0.50 |
| OpenAI 2023, GPT-4 | 0.56 | 0.59 |
| Claude 2026, exposure | 0.59 | 0.48 |
| Claude 2026, end-to-end autonomy | 0.55 | 0.63 |

Agreement on yes/no: human vs GPT-4 (2023) 82% (kappa 0.64); Claude 2026 vs human 80% (kappa 0.60); Claude 2026 vs GPT-4 84% (kappa 0.69). Repeat run of 300 tasks: same yes/no 89%, same autonomy level 78% (99.7% within one), same barrier 77%. How the 2023 "no" aged: of 1,376 desk tasks both raters called not doable, 26% had been seen in use by 2026 and 25% were rated doable in 2026.

Ladder conversions used by the translator: of desk tasks rated doable in 2023 (either rater), 42% were seen in use by May 2026 (26% of those rated not doable); of seen desk tasks, AI mostly does the work in 41% (mean automation share 48%); the share of Singapore firms using AI is read from IMDA's latest year and the central half-way year from the adoption curve; a "jobs" claim is first scaled by the median share of an occupation's task weight that is seen, among occupations with any seen task (27%). The translator multiplies a claim's number down the ladder from the rung the report is on; it is a historical conversion, not a forecast for that report. The scorecard on the same view lists every source behind the map with its rung, date and track record here.

## 5. Rung five: what has happened to jobs so far (`outcomes.py`)

The ladder's last rung is the job market itself. MOM publishes, by occupation, the things a disruption would eventually show up in, and the build now loads them (`python -m onetx fetch` downloads them; the MOM server needs the same case-variant retries as the wage files):

| What | Source | Detail | Period |
|---|---|---|---|
| Job vacancies | `mrsd_<year>_jv_by_ssoc_ind_occ_edu.xlsx`, sheet "Occupational Group" | SSOC 5-digit (2023 and 2024 as the share of vacancies within each occupational group, SSOC 2020 codes; 2025 as numbers, SSOC 2024 codes) | September 2023, 2024, 2025 |
| Employed residents | `mrsd_69_Emp_Res_DetailedOcc_Sex.xlsx` | SSOC sub-major group (2-digit), thousands | June, every year 2011 to 2025 |
| Median gross monthly wage | OWS June 2024 (title-matched, exact matches only) and June 2025 (coded) | SSOC 5-digit | 2024, 2025 |
| Retrenchments per 1,000 employees, job vacancy rate, resignation rate | `mrsd_33`, `mrsd_24`, `mrsd_26` time-series tables | three occupational groups (PMET; clerical, sales and service; production, transport, cleaners and labourers) | yearly to 2025 |

`outcomes.build_outcomes` gives every SSOC 2024 code: vacancies in September 2025; its minor group's share of its occupational group's vacancies in 2023 and 2025 (2025 numbers are converted to shares among listed occupations, 2023 shares are carried from SSOC 2020 to 2024 minor groups through the correspondence table); the wage change 2024 to 2025; its sub-major group's employment change 2019 to 2025 and 2023 to 2025; and its occupational group's retrenchment and vacancy rates. The page shows these under "What has happened so far" on every job, with the sub-group's employment series as a sparkline.

`outcomes.outcome_tests` then asks whether the map's signals relate to these outcomes yet, with rank correlations (a later quarter-done year means less exposed, so its sign is flipped):

| Outcome | Signal | n | Spearman | Reading |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| wage growth, Jun 2024 to Jun 2025 | AI doing it today | 450 | 0.10 | no clear sign yet |
| wage growth, Jun 2024 to Jun 2025 | AI doing by 2030 | 450 | 0.14 | no clear sign yet |
| vacancy share within group, 2023 to 2025 | AI doing it today | 627 | -0.09 | no clear sign yet |
| vacancy share within group, 2023 to 2025 | AI doing by 2030 | 627 | -0.10 | no clear sign yet |
| sub-group employment, 2023 to 2025 | AI doing by 2030 | 41 | 0.33 | the opposite of what disruption would show: more exposed jobs are doing better so far |
| sub-group employment, 2019 to 2025 | AI doing by 2030 | 41 | 0.52 | the opposite of what disruption would show: more exposed jobs are doing better so far |

Readings: below 0.15 in absolute value is "no clear sign yet"; in the direction disruption would show (falling employment, wages or vacancies where exposure is high) it is an early or clear sign; the other way it is reported as the opposite of disruption. None of this is evidence for or against the forecast, whose central years mostly start in 2029; it is the baseline the next releases will be judged against, and the test re-runs on every build.

Caveats: vacancy and wage surveys cover establishments with 25 or more employees; some detailed occupations are withheld for confidentiality, so within-group shares are among listed occupations; employment by detailed occupation is published only at the sub-major level; the 2024 wage match is by title and only exact matches are used.
